# Chunk 017 core-theme hits

Flagging policy: this file separates what is directly visible/legible from what still requires close transcription. Printed and transactional documents can support relatively strong claims; mounted-slip and handwritten dossiers are treated more conservatively.

## Tier 1 — Huizinga explicitly theorizes portrait evidence (`p0143–p0147`)

This sequence is one of the clearest first-order methodological statements in the reviewed corpus.

- `p0143` opens with the explicit subject: **the portrait of Philippe/Philip the Good**. The notes situate Burgundian dynastic portraiture among works associated with Claus Sluter, Hubert van Eyck and Rogier de la Pasture / van der Weyden, then turn to the multiple surviving exemplars of Philip's portrait.
- `p0144` asks the audience to inspect photographs of several copies. Madrid, Gotha, Antwerp, Lille and other variants are compared as members of a repeated portrait type. The page then asks what these portraits, **taken together and not simply as works of art**, can tell a historian interested in historical character.
- `p0145` explicitly rejects a literary/physiognomic procedure in which the observer reads a person's character and passions directly out of a portrait. Huizinga treats such inference as historically unproductive and instead frames the material as **sources iconographiques**.
- `p0146` states the methodological problem more sharply. Beside pictorial representation, famous princes normally leave a written tradition concerning person, character and physique. Both the **tradition iconographique** and the **tradition écrite** are defective and more or less fortuitous; historical information depends on the accident of **what has been preserved**.
- `p0147` asks whether the pictorial and written domains can be brought together to form a more convincing historical image, and then turns toward written witnesses such as Chastellain.

Research value: visual evidence is not treated here as illustration or as transparent physiognomy. It is one historical tradition among others, with its own copying history and preservation bias, to be triangulated against written evidence. This is unusually direct evidence for Huizinga's visual method.

## Tier 1 — portrait reproduction as a transaction chain (`p0203–p0208`)

The late dossier exposes the material route by which portrait reproductions could enter Huizinga's working papers.

### `p0203–p0204` — identifying image locations

Mounted research notes collect portrait bibliography, copy relationships and locations. `p0204` visibly includes **Gotha no. 78**, Lille, Madrid and other museum/catalogue references. Its OCR is pathological and cannot be quoted continuously, but its research function as a portrait-location/reference dossier is clear.

### `p0208` — direct request to a museum

A handwritten draft dated Leiden, November 1929 is addressed to the director of the museum in Gotha. Huizinga asks for a **good photograph, approximately 20 × 26 cm**, of the portrait/image of **Philip the Good of Burgundy**, identified by museum number (OCR gives no. 78).

### `p0205` — specialist photographic supplier in Gotha

An invoice to Huizinga from **Selma Jandt, vormals M. Jink & Sohn**, Auguststraße 7, Gotha, dated **27 November 1929**, identifies the business as a photographic establishment and advertises enlargements by different processes up to life size. The invoice text visibly concerns a photographic item relating to Philip the Good of Burgundy. This is direct evidence of commercial photographic production attached to the research dossier.

### `p0206` — art-reproduction infrastructure in Lille

A second invoice, dated **5 December 1929**, is addressed to **J. Huizinga, professeur d'histoire, 32 Witte Singel, Leiden**, from **M. Escoute**, 58 rue Jacquemars-Giélée, Lille. The printed heading advertises **PHOTOGRAPHIE INDUSTRIELLE ET DOCUMENTAIRE** and **REPRODUCTIONS D'ART**, with preparation of catalogues, enlargements and reductions. The bill itemizes a `cliché 20 × 26`, an `épreuve 20 × 26`, travel/displacement time and **two proofs deposited obligatorily at the museum**.

The Lille document is unquestionably a Huizinga art-reproduction transaction. The surviving page does not by itself securely establish that its depicted object is the same Gotha portrait, so the two transactions should remain distinct unless another page closes that link.

### `p0207` — payment infrastructure

Dutch **BUITENLANDSCHE POSTWISSEL / BEWIJS VAN STORTING** receipts are physically mounted with the surrounding research notes. Their presence preserves the cross-border payment layer that connects image acquisition to postal/financial infrastructure.

Operational chain securely visible across the cluster:

`identify portrait/location → contact museum → commission photographic reproduction → specialist photographic / art-reproduction service → cliché / proof / enlargement-reduction infrastructure → postal payment and museum-deposit copies → research photograph`

This materially extends the production-and-circulation evidence from previous chunks: here the archive preserves not only a reproduction but the request, supplier, format, production process, payment and institutional routing around it.

## Tier 1 — archival correction feeds back into character history (`p0218–p0221`)

A printed offprint/extract, **“Quatre lettres autographes de Philippe le Bon”**, is accompanied on `p0218` by a handwritten note explaining that it is sent to rectify an earlier statement in an article on the **character of Philippe le Bon** that no letters of his survived.

The printed article reports the discovery, during research on Philip's crusading projects, of **four notes entirely in the duke's own hand** in the `Kleve-Mark` holdings of the Düsseldorf State Archives. Its methodological claim is explicit: the letters are valuable less because their historical matter is new than because their wording throws sharp light on the **character of their author**; without a secretary's protocol, the writer's personality is said to appear more directly. `p0219–p0221` contextualize and transcribe the letters.

Research value: the historical `image` of Philip is shown as corrigible. New archival evidence can be sent into an existing scholarly argument and alter claims about character. Combined with `p0143–p0147`, the dossier gives a particularly strong sequence:

`iconographic tradition + written descriptive tradition + newly recovered autograph evidence → revised historical character image`

## Tier 2 — the internal `beeld van Phil. den Goed` dossier (`p0180–p0204`)

`p0180` explicitly begins **`het beeld van Phil. den Goed`** and the following mounted boards aggregate character descriptors, quotations, bibliography, portrait references and object/location notes. Many pages are OCR-poor because the board layout destroys reading order.

The safest research claim is material rather than textual: the dossier constructs a historical `beeld` by physically juxtaposing heterogeneous fragments. This makes the archive itself part of the synthetic operation. Exact quotation from much of `p0180–p0204` requires close transcription.

## Tier 2 — copy/original genealogy and iconographic value (`p0224–p0225`)

The German art-historical extract describes a portrait of Philip the Good and then addresses the evidentiary status of portraits that cannot claim to be original because of anachronisms between the codex and the lives of some sitters. The text nevertheless argues that such images retain value for **Ikonographie** when they are early and carefully executed.

It then compares the manuscript miniature with an older panel portrait and proposes that neither need be a direct copy of the other: both may descend from a **third, still older original** that is no longer known.

Research value: this gives the dossier an explicit genealogy-of-copies problem. A non-original image may still carry historical evidence, but only through reconstruction of transmission and descent. That problem closely matches Huizinga's own concern in `p0143–p0147` with multiple copies and an imperfect iconographic tradition.

## Tier 2 — diagrammatic working image (`p0047`)

`p0047` contains a hand-drawn layout with boxed arrangements, human figures and annotations. The OCR captures only a small fraction of the page. The exact referents are not secure enough to interpret further, but the visual form is important: as in chunk 016, Huizinga's working archive contains diagrammatic organization produced within the dossier itself, alongside externally supplied reproductions.
