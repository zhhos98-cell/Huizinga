# Only a Benevolent Outsider
## Johan Huizinga, Bronisław Malinowski, and the Waning of the Primitive, 1926–1933

By August 1931, five years after their American encounter, Johan Huizinga had read Bronisław Malinowski for years, exchanged work with him, and helped frame a proposal for a Centre of Anthropological Research at Leiden. Asked who could give the centre a ‘central aim or principle’, he answered: ‘I myself am only a benevolent outsider.’[^1]

The phrase is harder to place than it looks. Huizinga had used Malinowski in teaching and medieval history; Malinowski had read Huizinga and praised his historical approach; Huizinga was now assembling an anthropological project. Why did proximity produce an outsider?

*Primitive* is part of the problem, though not as one stable concept shared by both men. Malinowski wrote of savage and primitive societies; Huizinga of ‘so-called primitive culture’, a ‘stage of savage life’, and *primitief* in quite different historical and philological settings. What joined these uses was not a common definition but a recurring problem of historical inference: what could relations observed among peoples described as primitive disclose about other times and forms of life? Malinowski increasingly answered by unpacking such categories into reciprocity, obligation, knowledge and practice; Huizinga asked what those relations could bear when carried into historical comparison. The word travelled easily. What it could authorize became harder to assume.

In the modern human sciences, *primitive* had acquired a particularly consequential historical use. Difference visible in the present could be made to stand for anteriority in human history: a living people, custom or object could be classified as primitive and thereby positioned nearer an earlier phase of human development (Fabian, 1983; Banerjee, 2006; Pels, 2022). Classification could therefore carry a temporal inference with it. The conceptual history at stake here is the changing force of that inference. By the interwar period the vocabulary remained widespread, while increasingly dense descriptions of law, mentality, exchange and culture placed more steps between the label and the conclusion.

This article deliberately borrows Huizinga’s own language of waning as a second-order historical term. The borrowing comes from the self-reflexive cautions in the 1919 preface to *Herfsttij*. Huizinga warned that attention fixed on decline could cast ‘te veel van de schaduw des doods’ over the work, and ended by asking whether historical inquiry could approach ‘den wezenlijken inhoud ... die in die vormen heeft gerust’ (Huizinga, 1919). He had opened the same preface by criticizing the habit of viewing each period above all for the promises it held for the next. *Waning* here names a problem of precursor history, death-shadow, and the relation between forms and what they contain.

Writing *Mijn weg tot de historie* in 1943, Huizinga offered a second retrospective perspective. He remembered reading and excerpting Max Müller as unquestionable wisdom before Tylor’s *Primitive Culture* opened vistas that remained ‘kiemen van mijn wetenschappelijk denken’, even though its animist thesis had long become obsolete and been abandoned (Huizinga, 1947). His own intellectual genealogy thus ran through the nineteenth-century comparative world in which origins, living difference and cultural sequence had been drawn together. The doctrine could become obsolete while some of the questions and habits it had organized remained. An intellectual form could lose authority without disappearing from a later repertoire.

Recent scholarship shows that the interwar *primitive* was not fading into obscurity. The emerging human sciences made it increasingly visible and operational: in books and courses, disputes over law and mentality, philanthropic programmes, museum classification and institutional design (Biehn, 2009; Chimisso, 2000; Pels, 2022). Recent work on Malinowski has similarly weakened the familiar story of a sudden passage from evolutionism to functionalism, describing a refocusing towards intensive local study rather than a disciplinary reset (Lagerspetz and Suolinna, 2025). The question here is what the surviving category could still settle without further relations, evidence and disciplinary authority. The Huizinga–Malinowski exchange shifts that problem sideways, from succession within anthropology to movement between anthropology and history. The question could travel more easily than the support for an answer.

That shift exposes a gap in the literature. Most accounts stop at the disciplinary edge. They explain how anthropology reworked its own *primitive*; they say less about what happened when its questions entered another historical craft whose sources, comparisons and claims to authority were organized differently. The encounter matters precisely because neither man entered it as a stranger to *primitive*. The term belonged to a wider human-scientific atmosphere, but a shared vocabulary did not make its uses identical. This article follows the conclusions that *primitive* was made to carry across books, courses, letters, museum memoranda and historical comparisons, and asks how those conclusions were qualified, redistributed or withheld.

Recent Huizinga scholarship has also insisted on putting his historical work back inside the pressures of his own present. Rydin (2024) reads that work through interwar rupture and loss; the pair archive extends the historicization into the reorganizing human sciences, where books, foundations, museums and disciplinary projects became sites of choice. Recent intellectual history has already moved from older models of origins and influence toward reception, circulation and active reuse (Wickberg, 2023); work on the ‘(non-)globalisation’ of ideas has gone further by treating actors’ decisions to appropriate or not appropriate an idea as causal historical action (Morgan, 2025). Here such choices become part of the history of a category: *primitive* could be retained while the inferences once compressed inside it were broken apart and reassigned.

## America, 1926: Primitive before the Encounter

During Huizinga’s spring 1926 American tour, his itinerary carried him through Philadelphia, Baltimore, Washington and Chapel Hill. At Harvard his route crossed Malinowski’s: Malinowski was himself in the United States on a Rockefeller-supported visit, and his report places Huizinga in a Harvard seminar where discussions similar to Malinowski’s theoretical programme were repeated.[^2]

The encounter met an existing repertoire. Huizinga later remembered reaching history from Sanskrit and comparative linguistics by a ‘salto mortale’; he also admitted that his early teaching preferred a vivid story to critical groundedness (Huizinga, 1947). Long before the American encounter, the 1919 first edition of *Herfsttij* had called symbolic thought, from an ethnological standpoint, `een zeer primitieve geestesfunctie` and characterized *het primitieve denken* by a weak perception of identity boundaries, using that ethnological model to account for medieval symbolism.[^24] His Indian papers also used *primitief* for earlier intellectual or medical layers.[^3] In 1921, reviewing Wells’s attempt to extend world history from planetary and biological development into human history, Huizinga objected that geological or biological *ontwikkeling* had little to do with ‘de functie van het historisch begrijpen’; a wider chronology did not by itself yield historical understanding (Huizinga, 1921). His critique of Spengler made the same problem comparative: exact homologies across civilizations forced particulars onto the Procrustean bed of a system, while *Preussentum und Sozialismus* turned the operation political through a *historisch-ethnografische tegenstelling* between English and Prussian character (Huizinga, 1921). On 7 April 1926, only days before his transatlantic crossing—and six days before a surviving letter places him aboard the *Berengaria*[^25]—he attacked *evolutie* as a general historical principle, defined cultural history as social morphology concerned with what connects people to one another rather than with what holds them together internally, and placed ethnology among the special cultural sciences already practising such morphology (Huizinga, 1927: 5–7). In the same theses he warned that general culture-historical terms could acquire a mythological element and suffer inflation, while period names that purported to state an era’s essence could prejudge and mislead (Huizinga, 1927: 5–7).

For *primitive*, these materials show a tension rather than a clean separation of position from explanation. In *Herfsttij*, the primitive model itself helped explain medieval symbolism, and verbal efficacy was said to appear in its fullness in primitive culture while persisting into the late Middle Ages.[^24] Elsewhere *primitief* could mark an earlier intellectual or medical layer; meanwhile Huizinga’s criticism of evolution denied that developmental sequence by itself supplied historical understanding. The pre-contact repertoire therefore contained both the older explanatory shortcut and a methodological reason not to let sequence or a general term settle the argument in advance. The functional language Huizinga encountered in America, Malinowski’s included, entered that tension: between locating a form and specifying the relations through which it worked.

On Malinowski’s side, the Rockefeller report gives the seminar a clearer place in his own programme. It linked anthropology to the practical influence of social science on policy and to the place of science in American life, and under ‘The functional view of Anthropology’ described ideas, customs and institutions through the work they performed within an integral cultural scheme. The report says that similar theoretical discussions were repeated at Harvard. By 19 May, writing home from Boston, Huizinga was already calling him ‘mijn nieuwen vriend Malinowski’ and promising to tell his son more about him after returning.[^2] In the same family letter he did not reduce the new acquaintance to a school or doctrine. Malinowski, he wrote, had points of resemblance with Jolles but also ‘groote verschillen’. The remark fixes a personal and comparative judgment before the surviving exchange of books: Huizinga had heard Malinowski in an American setting, continued to think about him after leaving Harvard, and was already placing resemblance beside difference.

Huizinga’s own American notes place the functional vocabulary in a wider setting. In *Amerika levend en denkend*, internally dated October 1926 and published the following year, he described American social science as pluralist and hostile to a single explanatory ground. Summarizing Dewey, he wrote that all action was *interactie, wederzijdsche handeling* and that morality, economic life and scientific thought were *sociale functies*; at the same time he resisted making history mere *hand- en spandiensten* for sociology.[^22] By December, *functional* already belonged to a broader American repertoire; the pair exchange attached it to particular ethnological books and to Huizinga’s historical problems.

Histories of interwar anthropology place such encounters inside foundation patronage, where anthropologists had to articulate the distinctiveness of their objects and methods (Biehn, 2009). Five years later Malinowski would ask what could make Leiden’s resources anthropology.

## ‘Primitive Law’ and Historical Use, 1926–1929

By December, nearly seven months after the Boston letter, Huizinga was recalling another conversation about ‘ethnological problems’, this time along the Haarlemsche Trekvaart. That recollection appears in his letter of 12 December 1926. He thanked Malinowski for a letter followed by three books, ‘the small, the medium and the big one’, and recalled their earlier conversation while walking along the Haarlemsche Trekvaart. Huizinga began with *Crime and Custom in Savage Society*, made the calf joke, then turned serious: Malinowski’s ‘outlook on ethnological problems’ was ‘just the one I can profit by for my historical method’. Nineteen days later, in a second letter of 31 December, he made the affinity explicit: ‘We emphasize the functional side of cultural phenomena.’ Feudal law, troubadour culture and the efficacy of words followed immediately.[^4]

The order inside those two December letters matters. Malinowski did not send a single abstract programme. Huizinga received a small, a medium and a large book and made a reading choice among them, beginning with *Crime and Custom*. The Milo joke gives that choice a physical sequence: calf first, heavier animal later. Huizinga then returned from the book to their earlier walk and from the walk to his own historical craft. By 31 December the traffic had moved again. The formulation about ‘the functional side’ was followed not by a declaration of allegiance but by a string of historical objects—medieval law, feudal relations, troubadour culture, the force of words, and sentiments such as vanity, generosity and friendship. The force of words was not a new object: in the 1919 first edition of *Herfsttij*, Huizinga had already described belief in `de werking van het gesproken woord` as fully manifest in primitive culture and persisting in late-medieval blessings, magical formulas and legal formulas.[^24] What had arrived as books was already being redistributed across a historian’s problems.

Malinowski’s published self-description adds another source. Earlier in 1926, in the dedication to *Myth in Primitive Psychology*, adapted from his 1925 Frazer Lecture, he recalled illness interrupting physical and chemical research, *The Golden Bough*, and becoming ‘bound to the service of Frazerian anthropology’ (Malinowski, 1926b: 5–6). His early writings place that conversion story inside a broader Central European formation (Thornton and Skalník, 1993). *Crime and Custom* still named ‘primitive law’, but its account of how rules bound moved through reciprocal services, kin relations, public knowledge and obligations of return (Malinowski, 1926a). The title preserved a classificatory object while the body shifted the burden of explanation. Once the force of law had to be shown through reciprocity, obligation, public knowledge and practice, *primitive* no longer explained why law worked; it marked the field in which the problem was posed. His attack on ‘primitive mentality’ worked in a similar way: Trobriand agriculture, navigation and technical knowledge stood against allowing the label to determine the contents of mind in advance (Tilley, 2010). The category survived the argument, but its supposed contents had to be tested against situated knowledge and technique. The interwar controversy over ‘primitive mentality’ was also a dispute over disciplinary competence: claims about the universality or transformation of mind were tied to claims about the scientific status of ethnology itself (Chimisso, 2000).

For their exchange, the important move was from what primitive or savage societies were said to be toward what relations observed there could do in another historical argument. Huizinga’s 31 December phrase ‘so-called primitive culture’ is significant in this setting because the classification immediately opens onto other terms: vanity, generosity, friendship, words and feudal relations. Elsewhere a working slip called knightly games ‘beleving der Feodaltijd’.[^4] The slip shows the sort of historical form to which a question about social efficacy could attach. Malinowski’s ethnography did not supply the relational vocabulary from scratch. It bound older historical objects—generosity, friendship and the efficacy of words—to observed circuits of reciprocity, obligation, trust and exchange; Huizinga tested that denser configuration’s historical reach.[^17] *Primitive* remained at the entrance to the comparison while more of the explanatory work moved into what people did, expected, gave, said and owed.

Fifteen months after the 31 December letter, on 29 March 1928, *Sex and Repression in Savage Society* gave Huizinga ‘new visions concerning the very origins of culture’, while *Crime and Custom* remained perhaps more useful ‘for historical use’.[^5] The distinction is revealing. ‘Origins of culture’ retained an older promise attached to *primitive*: living difference could offer access to beginnings. ‘Historical use’ valued Malinowski after that shortcut had been loosened. Relations observed in the Trobriands could become useful to history without requiring the Trobrianders to stand simply as Europe’s past. The same year, the third edition of *Mensch en menigte in Amerika* described modern urban revivalism as `het voortleven van een primitieve geesteshouding in een economisch hoog ontwikkeld milieu` and compared its credulity and excitability with the medieval popular mind.[^23] Here *primitive* marked persistence inside modernity rather than the first position in a sequence. In the same letter Huizinga resisted one Freudian argument by invoking ‘the immense complexity of the smallest social phenomenon’. He then generalized about Freud and Marx through the antisemitic stereotype of a ‘Talmudic mind’. Complexity and flattening sat on the same page. The letter also sent Huizinga’s remarks on American culture to Malinowski for judgment.

Malinowski replied on 15 August, ‘We often speak of you’, and called the reading of Huizinga’s American notes ‘a most stimulating event’: ‘we read them aloud’.[^6] The reply makes the direction of travel concrete. A historian who had been receiving books on ‘savage society’ sent his own diagnosis of contemporary America back to the anthropologist; Malinowski’s household then made it an oral text. The exchange was therefore already bidirectional before Huizinga reused the Trobriands in teaching. That matters for *primitive*: the category and the relations attached to it did not move along a one-way line from an anthropological centre to a historical periphery. The same correspondence corridor was carrying Huizinga’s judgments of modern culture in the opposite direction.

Nine months later, on 13 May 1929, after receiving *The Sexual Life of Savages in North-Western Melanesia*, Huizinga said he felt ‘quite at home in the Trobriands’. He then described the course he had taught the previous winter to students at Delft Technical University, organized around *homo faber*: it ran from ‘the stage of savage life’ through old Aryan culture to Rome and its decay.[^7] Thick familiarity and stadial order coexisted. Here the older work of *primitive* remained explicit: ‘savage life’ occupied the first position in a historical sequence. Yet the material filling that position now came from an ethnography that had made any simple stage internally dense. A stage could order materials in time while Malinowski’s descriptions specified relations within it. The two operations coexisted because they did different work.

Huizinga’s 1929 methodological writings add two related formulations. In ‘Over een definitie van het begrip geschiedenis’, ‘the primitive cultures of the whole world’ had become components of modern civilization; yet a history adequate to that civilization still had to answer to critically scientific verification (Huizinga, 1929: 156–168). The wording changes the temporal position of the object. The ‘primitive cultures of the whole world’ were not merely surviving beginnings outside modern history; they had become components of the civilization whose history Huizinga was trying to understand. A separate essay in the same volume, ‘De taak der cultuurgeschiedenis’, made the division of labour concrete: religious studies and ethnology could define forms such as myth, consecration, sacred action, contest-play and secret association, while cultural history followed their *werking* in historical events. Service, honour, loyalty and obedience might each be objects of sociology, but their systematic treatment remained insufficient unless cultural history showed their changing operation and form across centuries and countries. Cultural history could learn from ‘de praktische sociologie der Amerikanen’ and from Mauss without becoming subordinate labour for sociology (Huizinga, 1929). *Primitive* could still order difference, but it no longer supplied a self-explanatory outside from which modern history simply began.

## The Historical Work of *Primitief*, 1929–1931

Between the May 1929 letter and the July 1931 Leiden proposal, Huizinga’s own comparative work left a different documentary trail. In November 1929, while working on Philip the Good, Huizinga asked the museum in Gotha for a good photograph of the duke’s portrait. The surrounding dossier did not treat a portrait as a transparent access to character. Huizinga compared copies, reconstructed relations among versions, followed what museums and photographic suppliers could make available, and treated preservation itself as part of the historical problem. A newly recovered autograph letter could correct an existing historical image; a photographic reproduction was useful only within a transmission chain that could itself be checked.[^10] Working notes from the same period distinguish collecting or establishing historical material from ordering it under a point of view.[^11] This documentary labour matters to the history of *primitive* because it shows what happened after a comparison had been suggested. A category might place or connect an object; a historical claim still had to pass through transmission, copy, survival and correction.

By 1930, in his French manuscript on twelfth-century intellectual life, *Trois esprits prégothiques*, *Cet élément ludique* opens an argument about scholastic competition and disputation, and *primitive* appears in the continuation of that same argument; a few pages later the text refuses a simple Renaissance-precursor reading.[^9] A separate Renaissance packet begins ‘Met dien primitief begrip der Renaissance…’—*primitief* modifying a concept, not a people.[^3] Across these files the lexical family was doing several temporal jobs. It could mark an earlier stratum, a rudimentary concept, or—in anthropology—a class of living peoples and cultures placed in relation to human beginnings. The same word therefore did not guarantee the same temporal relation. Its portability made the inference attached to each use more, rather than less, important.

The interval also included Huizinga’s 1930–31 journey to Java, Bali and Hong Kong. Writing retrospectively in *Mijn weg tot de historie*, he contrasted a journey remembered ‘entirely’ from memory after an injury to his right eye with the day-to-day notes of his 1926 American trip (Huizinga, 1947). The surviving American archive shows what those earlier ‘notes’ meant: cards on frontier history, Indian policy and the Ghost Dance lead into comparative prose on America and Europe.[^8] Malinowski’s published ethnography rested on observation of living relations; Huizinga moved through notes, memory, cards, reproductions and surviving texts. Both made distant worlds portable, but the evidentiary work behind that portability differed. For a category such as *primitive*, which could turn geographical or cultural distance into historical distance, those differences mattered. In the materials traced here, relations described through field observation and historical claims assembled through damaged textual or visual transmission passed through different kinds of checking.

Two strands of Huizinga scholarship bear directly on this material. One concerns source practice: work on the making of *Herfsttij* has already made Huizinga’s selective source use and material organization objects of historiography (Small, 2019; van der Lem, 2019). The later archive extends that problem across cards, travel memory, photographs, autograph letters and surviving texts. A second concerns anthropology. Arnade (2019) emphasizes anthropological affinities in Huizinga’s treatment of ritual and cultural form; Vale (2021) warns that an *anthropologist avant la lettre* can obscure the philological and linguistic formation that continued to shape his historical work. The correspondence places direct anthropological exchange alongside the historian’s continuing philological checks. Taken together, the archive makes visible why the mobility of *primitive* did not imply a common method: the word could cross fields more easily than the evidence needed to make its temporal implications persuasive.

## ‘Primitive or Semi-Cultured’: Leiden, 1931

The memorandum did not begin its life when Huizinga sent it abroad. On 17 July Van Vollenhoven told him that the paper had already been shown locally, including to Van Eerde, and revised; Snouck Hurgronje was proposed as another reader.[^12] The object that reached Malinowski was therefore already a Leiden composite, altered by local circulation before it entered the Huizinga–Malinowski correspondence. That chronology matters because the classificatory language in the packet was not merely an eccentric private vocabulary. It belonged to a practical attempt to gather existing museums, institutes, languages and colonial expertises into an institution.

Huizinga had already put part of that institutional map into print. In June 1929, discussing university ‘role division’, he reproduced the claim that Leiden’s chairs in Indonesian languages and in *land- en volkenkunde van Nederlandsch Indië* were ‘een privilege der Leidsche Universiteit’, explicitly linked to the university’s collections and chairs in colonial law.[^20]

Two days later, on 19 July 1931, Huizinga sent Malinowski ‘a paper which I made up’ for a Centre of Anthropological Research. The letter followed a visit by Van Sickle and Tracy Kittredge, who had come ‘to look into the matter’.[^12] Leiden already possessed an ethnographical museum, Oriental and language institutes, legal and *adat* expertise tied to colonial administration, and other dispersed scholarly resources; Huizinga asked Malinowski for judgment and, if favourable, Rockefeller help. He allowed for more than one route: Malinowski might be asked formally for an opinion, or he might ‘drop in a word unasked’. If Malinowski did not approve, Huizinga asked him to treat the request as though it had not been made. The supporting packet comprised Huizinga’s seven-page signed memorandum and a two-page signed annex by A. W. Nieuwenhuis.[^12]

The papers placed Leiden’s resources on a civilizational map. Java could be ‘high culture’, Bali an unusually interesting Hindu remnant, other populations a ‘backward stage’. Nieuwenhuis’s annex went further: ‘Ethnographical objects as data for psychological research about primitive or semi-cultured races’.[^13] Wicker-work, textiles, metalwork and carving could become evidence of mental capacities; those capacities could bear on development, administration and indigenous industry. Here *primitive* recovered a harder classificatory force. The annex proposed a route from objects to mental capacities, from capacities to developmental position, and from developmental position to practical questions of administration and indigenous industry. Present difference was again being asked to bear developmental and historical weight. Huizinga’s 1926 warning about general historical terms had therefore not produced a steady retreat from classification: the annex was Nieuwenhuis’s, but Huizinga helped assemble and forward a packet in which developmental labels still did practical work. Pels’s work on classification helps situate this: categories could describe objects, organize research and structure intervention at the same time (Pels, 2022).

Huizinga’s own 1930 lectures set a limit from another direction. In a lecture sequence on seventeenth-century Dutch civilization he admitted social, economic, political and, ‘if one wishes’, ethnographic conditions, then denied that their enumeration completely explained a civilization.[^11]

Eleven days after Huizinga sent the memorandum, Malinowski replied on 30 July. The order he gave the events is unusually revealing. Van Sickle had already spoken to him about Leiden during a recent weekend stay; with only that briefing Malinowski had expressed unreserved interest and enthusiasm. After the memorandum itself arrived, however, support became conditional on reading. He still said he would gladly do what he could and still intended to write to the Rockefeller side, but he postponed that intervention until he had made ‘a few comments on your Memorandum’ and heard Huizinga’s answer.[^14]

The comments turned immediately to constitution. Who would ‘carry on the work and inspire the institution with their spirit’? What ‘central aim or principle’ would hold it together? Malinowski worried that museum buildings and equipment dominated the scheme, rejected anthropology as a *mixtum compositum* of archaeology, collectioneering and other activities, asked for ‘a definite social science’, urged Leiden to ‘sociologise’, and joined living language to living culture. His judgment of Huizinga himself was equally explicit: ‘Your method in history strikes me as being the right sort of really anthropological approach, so that if you could play even a teaching part it would be invaluable.’[^14] The sequence is not simply enthusiasm followed by criticism. Van Sickle’s oral briefing produced enthusiasm; Huizinga’s paper produced an audit; the audit temporarily changed what Malinowski was prepared to do with Rockefeller.

Reading a packet that classified peoples, objects and capacities, Malinowski redirected the question toward the relations and personnel that would make those classifications count as anthropology. Who would do the work? What principle connected museum, archaeology, language and social science? Where would living language and living culture enter? The categories and collections could identify an object of research; they could not by themselves supply the anthropology Malinowski wanted.

Thirteen days later, on 12 August, Huizinga answered by conceding the ‘weakest spots in our scheme’. He admitted that the memorandum contained ‘a bit of window-dressing, or rather of a draft on the future’. The local mechanism, as he described it, was the reorganization of the Ethnographical Museum and closer contact with the Eastern institutes, through which a centre might emerge; making the movement toward sociology more explicit would scare ‘our own men’, though he personally welcomed that direction. Leiden’s leading *adat* scholar was ‘no anthropologist proper’. ‘I myself am only a benevolent outsider.’[^1]

Here the history of *primitive* and the outsider converge. Huizinga could assemble the museums, languages, colonial legal expertise and civilizational classifications through which the category operated, and Malinowski could call his historical method ‘really anthropological’. Yet the proposed centre still lacked the disciplinary principle and personnel Malinowski demanded. The shared category crossed the boundary more easily than authority over its use. Chimisso’s account of the interwar dispute over ‘primitive mentality’ offers a useful comparison: disputes over an object could become disputes over which discipline was competent to speak for it (Chimisso, 2000). In Leiden that question was attached to a proposed centre, its personnel and its programme.

The audit did not stop the traffic between them. On 15 September an unpublished Yale letter from Huizinga to Malinowski survives with the incipit ‘I am sending you a volume just pubished’.[^15] Its body remains uncontrolled in the repository, so the identity of the dispatch cannot be made certain. Nineteen days later, however, Malinowski’s controlled reply of 4 October opened by thanking Huizinga for the newly published *Encyclopaedie der Karaïben*. He had already passed it into current work: a pupil studying the couvade was using it. Malinowski added that, given enough time and a dictionary, he could read Dutch.

The same 4 October letter carried two other routes in the opposite direction. Malinowski enclosed a Bertrand Russell review of Huizinga’s book in case he wanted or needed a copy. He also wrote that he was taking Huizinga’s 12 August institute letter to Paris and would raise the Leiden matter with Van Sickle there if necessary.[^15] Thus one page records a book moving from Huizinga through Malinowski to a pupil, a review moving back from Malinowski to Huizinga, and Huizinga’s institutional letter moving onward through Malinowski toward the Rockefeller network. The July–August dispute over what counted as anthropology had not ended the corridor. It had made its distribution of authority more explicit.

## Coda: What Carried the Comparison, 1932–1933

By 13 November 1932, thirteen months after the October 1931 letter, the exchange was still active. Malinowski thanked Huizinga for sending his article on the relation of the poetic and the theological in Alanus de Insulis, and three days later Huizinga answered him. The article itself asked how Alanus’s allegorical figures stood between conscious conviction and `gedankenlose Spielerei`, insisting that the two did not lie cleanly separated in the human mind.[^18] Malinowski replied again on 27 November. Huizinga wrote on 1 December about Malinowski’s health; on 3 December the correspondence inventory records two further letters, one in each direction. The surviving record therefore contains six direct letters in little more than three weeks. What had looked like a thin coda to the 1931 institutional dispute was in fact another concentrated period of scholarly and personal exchange.

On 30 January 1933, in an unpublished Yale letter, Malinowski told Huizinga that he underestimated his Dutch, said he was willing to write for the Dutch periodical *De Gids*, and considered beginning in Dutch and having Huizinga correct his style.[^18] The exchange had therefore become linguistically intimate as well as intellectually reciprocal.

The same density frames the disciplinary limit in 1933. On 20 May Malinowski thanked Huizinga for a letter and a reprint. Three days later, when Malinowski stood for the Royal Netherlands Academy, Huizinga wrote: ‘Being no anthropologist I refrained from taking a leading part in your candidature’, though he signed it.[^16] The proximity matters: non-leadership was stated inside an active exchange of texts, not after communication had gone cold.

Later that year, in his rectoral address *Over de grenzen van spel en ernst in de cultuur*, Huizinga moved from the Trobriand Kula exchange to Mauss’s potlatch and then to Philip the Good’s 1454 Burgundian *Vœu du Faisan*. The published text makes the bridge narrower than a stack of parallels. Huizinga said he was adding the Burgundian case not for its ‘play-character in general’ but as a specific example of *wedijverend, wederkeerig weeldevertoon en spilziek onthaal*: a sequence of court banquets in which hosts took turns, passed a wreath, escalated splendour, and were finally outdone by Philip. Only after specifying that sequence did he call the case ‘not so very far’ from a potlatch.[^21] Semitic and Sanskrit play vocabulary then returned; Genoa and Antwerp brought wagering and calculation. The comparison remained heterogeneous: Kula remained Kula, Burgundy remained Burgundy, and the old philologist remained inside the historian.

Late 1933 adds another route, this time returning explicitly to *primitive*. On 27 October Huizinga wrote Malinowski that he had come across a review of Sjoerd Hofstra’s *Differenzierungserscheinungen in einigen afrikanischen Gruppen: Ein Beitrag zur Frage der primitiven Individualität*; Malinowski replied three days later.[^19] The Amsterdam lecture Huizinga delivered on 6 November began from a problem of naming. The great objects of cultural history, he wrote, were so complex and diffuse that they could not properly bear a name or be grasped as a closed unity, yet without names history could not make them visible to the mind; such names often arose from the wishes and imagination of the age itself (Huizinga, 1934). He then wrote that when humanity means the future it often cries ‘back’, and footnoted Hofstra’s citation of W. C. Willoughby’s prophet among *natuurvolken* calling people to return to old loyalties because the ideal age lay in the past. The ethnographic example supplied a relation—return to an idealized past—to a general historical claim. The people described as primitive did not have to stand simply as Europe’s past.

The verb ‘refrained’ records an action as much as a limit. Histories of intellectual exchange readily follow successful uptake—what was borrowed, adopted, transmitted or institutionalized—but this correspondence also preserves choices not to complete a transfer. Malinowski postponed his promised Rockefeller intervention after reading the Leiden memorandum; Huizinga helped assemble the centre while declining to claim the anthropological leadership it lacked, and in 1933 supported Malinowski’s candidature while deliberately not taking a leading part. Movement and self-limitation belonged to the same exchange.

In the 1933 address itself, Huizinga frames comparison as a boundary problem. After Kula and potlatch he asks where seriousness ends and play begins, then turns to wagering and calculation in Genoa and Antwerp (Huizinga, 1933). Ethnology, medieval court culture, philology and economic history remain in the same comparison; play is another question moving among them.

The contrast between 1929 and 1933 is visible in what carried the comparison. In 1929 ‘the stage of savage life’ could itself organize a historical sequence.[^7] In 1933 Huizinga moved through circulation, obligation, prestige, rivalry and play, and Burgundy entered the comparison without being assigned to a primitive stage (Huizinga, 1933). The late Hofstra episode makes the point sharper: *primitive* still circulated between Huizinga and Malinowski, but Huizinga’s historical use of the ethnographic material turned on a specific orientation toward the past rather than on a simple equivalence between contemporary ‘primitive’ peoples and an earlier European stage. The category remained available while less of the comparison depended on the category alone. This is the waning traced here.

Locher later argued that Malinowski had not modified Huizinga’s global image of primitive culture (Locher, 1958). The correspondence makes that judgment compatible with change at another level. A large classificatory image could persist while the conclusions once compressed inside it were increasingly carried by specific relations, source checks and disciplinary distinctions. The question is therefore not whether Malinowski replaced Huizinga’s primitive, but what the surviving category still did by itself.

This is also why the article stops at 1933. From 1934 onward the play-centred line expands into a different corpus, while the political problem of culture belongs to a later body of evidence. Following either line would require another archive. The direct Huizinga–Malinowski exchange through late October 1933 shows the point at which a long-lived classificatory vocabulary remained present while its old temporal shortcut had become increasingly crowded by named relations, source checks and disciplinary distinctions.

The later twentieth-century encounter between anthropology and social history offers a useful contrast. Anthropological exemplars could remake historians’ questions and accounts of social change (Foks, 2024). The exchange reconstructed here belongs to an earlier, less settled configuration. Anthropology could already reorganize historical questions without transferring disciplinary authority. The later anthropological turn can make this earlier exchange look like a precursor. The archive points instead to a different configuration: by 1933 questions could move far across the boundary while disciplinary authority remained uneven.

Leiden had already given this waning an institutional form. Shared questions could move between anthropology and history; authority over their comparison did not move so easily. ‘Benevolent outsider’ was Huizinga’s name for that position. The word *primitive* remained. More and more of the proof stood outside it.
