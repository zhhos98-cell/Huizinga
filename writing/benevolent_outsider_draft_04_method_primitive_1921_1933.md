# Only a Benevolent Outsider
## Johan Huizinga, Bronisław Malinowski, and the Waning of the Primitive, 1926–1933

By August 1931 Johan Huizinga had been reading Bronisław Malinowski for years, exchanging work with him, and helping to frame a proposed Centre of Anthropological Research at Leiden. When Malinowski asked who could lead the centre and give it a “central aim or principle,” Huizinga answered: “I myself am only a benevolent outsider.”[^1]

The phrase is odd because Huizinga was hardly outside the traffic of anthropology. Malinowski had sent him books; Huizinga had used them in teaching and medieval history; Malinowski had read Huizinga’s own work and called his historical approach anthropological. Huizinga was now helping to assemble an anthropological institution. Why, at the point of greatest proximity, did he name himself an outsider?

The answer was partly institutional. A centre needed leaders, personnel and a principle that could turn collections, languages, law and ethnography into anthropology. It was also methodological. Huizinga could carry an anthropological question into history without treating ethnographic and historical evidence as interchangeable. Malinowski could praise that movement while still asking who counted as an anthropologist proper. “Outsider” named both limits at once.

The contested word *primitive* makes those limits visible. It did not disappear from their exchange. Malinowski still wrote of savage and primitive societies; Huizinga could write of “so-called primitive culture,” teach a “stage of savage life,” and help circulate a Leiden memorandum whose annex spoke of “primitive or semi-cultured races.” Yet the word increasingly failed to do all the explanatory work that such language once promised. Law had to be described through obligation and reciprocity; exchange through prestige and partnership; historical analogy through surviving texts, objects and chronology. The form remained while more of the argument moved elsewhere.

That persistence is better described as a waning than as a death. Huizinga had used *Herfsttij* to resist histories that looked at an old civilization only for the new world supposedly emerging from it. Old forms, he wrote, could remain fully developed even as they stiffened and lost their former relation to a living core; he also warned against casting too much of the shadow of death over a waning world.[^2] The history followed here is similarly untidy. *Primitive* remained vigorous in vocabulary and institutions while its inferential jurisdiction was being redistributed.

Recent histories of the human sciences have made this problem easier to state. The central question is no longer simply whether a scholar accepted or rejected an evolutionary doctrine. It is what a category authorized the scholar to infer: when a living population could stand for an earlier human past; when a local practice could represent “primitive philosophy”; when classification described an object and when it helped constitute one. Histories of classification, comparative religion and evidentiary standards have increasingly treated those licenses themselves as historical objects.[^3] Huizinga and Malinowski met while one such license was becoming harder to use automatically. The question could travel between them more easily than the warrant.

## I. America, 1926

The first scene is not a book but a journey. Huizinga crossed the Atlantic in April 1926. Malinowski’s report on his own American visit for the Laura Spelman Rockefeller Memorial records theoretical discussion at a Harvard seminar attended by R. B. Dixon, Alfred Tozzer, Marcel Mauss and “Professor Huizinga.” The report presented “the functional view of Anthropology” inside a larger argument about anthropology as an integrating social science. Rockefeller officers were simultaneously asking how social research might be organized, supported and put to use.[^4]

It would be easy to make this an origin scene: the historian encounters modern social science and acquires a new comparative method. Huizinga’s own retrospective account makes that reading difficult. Long before America he had thought of himself as a “linguist and Sanscritist.” Sanskrit under J. S. Speyer had led him through Indian texts and religion; comparative linguistics had taken him to Leipzig. He later described his arrival in a university history chair as a “salto mortale” after years spent in “the East and linguistics.” He also admitted that the move did not instantly make him a critical historian: as a young teacher he cared more for a vivid historical story than for critical groundedness.[^5] His later method was therefore neither an original philological essence nor an anthropological acquisition. It had been assembled out of earlier practices and later corrections.

By 1926 that assembly had acquired a sharper public form. A week before leaving for America Huizinga told the Historisch Genootschap that *evolutie* had become a damaging general principle in cultural history. He described cultural history as a kind of social morphology and explicitly placed ethnology among the neighbouring sciences that had long studied social forms.[^6] His older Indian working papers supply a useful control. They already use *primitief* and *primitieve* when distinguishing layers in Indian intellectual and medical materials. Malinowski did not give Huizinga the word *primitive*.[^7]

Nor did Huizinga need Malinowski in order to distrust an all-purpose historical sequence. In 1921 he had criticized H. G. Wells for allowing a preferred line of progress to select what counted in religious history, while his criticism of Spengler accepted morphological comparison but resisted a morphology that decided the significance of particulars in advance.[^8] These objections did not abolish sequence or morphology. They limited what either could prove. That distinction matters because Huizinga would continue to use stadial language after reading Malinowski.

Malinowski arrived in the Harvard room with a different mature problem. His American report treated institutions as parts of an “integral” cultural scheme and anthropology as a science capable of coordinating several kinds of social knowledge. Five years later he would ask the same organizational question of Leiden: what principle could make a set of resources into anthropology? The American setting therefore did not create either man’s method. It placed two already formed but unfinished scholarly practices inside the same machinery of comparison, funding and institutional design.

Their discussion did not end in Cambridge, Massachusetts. In December Huizinga remembered talking with Malinowski about “ethnological problems” while walking along the Haarlemsche Trekvaart. By then America had become correspondence, and correspondence soon became books.

## II. Books and Letters, 1926–1929

Malinowski sent Huizinga three books in December 1926. Huizinga called them “the small, the medium and the big one” and began with *Crime and Custom in Savage Society*. The joke in his reply came from Milo of Croton, who trained by carrying a calf until he could carry a bull: Malinowski, Huizinga suggested, meant to accustom him to anthropology the same way. Then the tone changed. Malinowski’s “outlook on ethnological problems” was “just the one I can profit by for my historical method.” Before the month ended Huizinga wrote again: “We emphasize the functional side of cultural phenomena.” Medieval law, feudal relations, troubadour culture and the efficacy of words followed almost immediately.[^9]

Malinowski had only recently told his own origin story in a very different register. Opening the 1925 Frazer Lecture, he remembered illness interrupting physical and chemical research, his discovery of *The Golden Bough*, and his becoming “bound to the service of Frazerian anthropology.”[^10] The story is revealing but too clean. His early writings show a longer Central European formation in which Ernst Mach, Nietzsche, Frazer and others all mattered before fieldwork made him famous.[^11] The point is not that Frazer was a false origin and Mach the true one. Malinowski’s mature anthropology grew through an older comparative vocabulary that it would increasingly make inadequate by itself.

*Crime and Custom* demonstrates the process. “Primitive law” remains the problem named by the book, but the adjective does not explain how Trobriand rules bind. Malinowski follows obligations through reciprocal services, kin relations, public knowledge and expectations of return. A breach matters because other relations are attached to it. The title still locates a recognized anthropological object; the explanation has migrated into the social relations surrounding it.

Huizinga responded in the same movement. His letter of 31 December names vanity, generosity and friendship in “so-called primitive culture,” but the phrase does less work than the examples around it. He immediately asks what makes a rule, a word or a social relation effective and carries the question back to feudal Europe. That movement cannot be described simply as Malinowski introducing a historian to primitive society. Huizinga already had *primitive* as a historical and philological term. What Malinowski supplied was a dense case in which the label ceased to be a sufficient account.

The exchange became more critical in 1928. After *Sex and Repression in Savage Society*, Huizinga praised “new visions concerning the very origins of culture” but still thought *Crime and Custom* perhaps more useful “for historical use.”[^12] The distinction is small and persistent. Origins attracted him; the strongest appropriation occurred when a problem could be carried back into historical work.

In the same letter Huizinga resisted Malinowski’s treatment of a Freudian problem. A historian, he wrote, was relatively proof against such reasoning because he knew “the immense complexity of the smallest social phenomenon.” The remark can sound like a declaration of historical anti-reductionism. The next sentences prevent that flattering reading. Huizinga generalized about Freud and Marx through an antisemitic stereotype of the “Talmudic mind.” His demand for complexity was real and unevenly applied. Historical discrimination did not immunize him against coarse classification.

Nor was the traffic one way. Huizinga sent Malinowski his own writing on America and asked for criticism. Malinowski replied in August: “We often speak of you”; Huizinga’s American notes had been “a most stimulating event,” and the household had read them aloud.[^13] This is evidence of reciprocal intellectual traffic, not symmetrical doctrinal influence. Malinowski had become a reader of Huizinga, but there is no comparable evidence that Huizinga reorganized functional anthropology as a theory.

The tension becomes almost visible on one page in May 1929. After receiving *The Sexual Life of Savages in North-Western Melanesia*, Huizinga told Malinowski that he felt “quite at home in the Trobriands.” He then described a Delft course on *homo faber* that began with “the stage of savage life,” moved to old Aryan culture, and ended with the Roman Empire and its decay. He had, he said, “profited immensely” from Malinowski.[^14]

Neither half of the letter cancels the other. Ethnographic familiarity did not dissolve stadial ordering. Stadial ordering did not prevent Trobriand life from becoming thick, specific and familiar. The older form remained, but it now contained more than its name could summarize. This is the waning that matters here. *Primitive* still arranged material; it could no longer settle the content of the material without the relations Malinowski had described.

Huizinga’s public account of cultural history in 1929 makes the boundary equally unstable. Ethnology and sociology belonged close to cultural history; recurrent forms such as myth, consecration, sacred action, combat-play and secret society could be defined across fields. Yet cultural history still asked how such forms appeared in particular historical circumstances and how their meanings changed.[^15] Crossing the boundary was ordinary work. The boundary had not therefore disappeared.

## III. Travel and the Work of Comparison

The letters about America were attached to a material practice. Huizinga’s surviving files contain cards and notes on Turner, Helen Hunt Jackson, James Mooney, the Ghost Dance, treaties, reservations and Indian Territory. Some lead into sustained comparisons between America and Europe. The exact packet Malinowski heard read aloud has not been identified, but the workbench is visible: travel became notes; notes accumulated readings; cards became comparative prose.[^16]

Huizinga later remembered the documentary difference between journeys with unusual precision. In 1926 he made notes from day to day during his American trip, though he refused to call them a diary. For the winter journey of 1930–31 to Java, Bali and Hong Kong, an injury to his right eye left him relying “entirely on my memory.”[^5] The contrast is more useful than another travel anecdote. The same traveller returned with knowledge made under different inscription regimes. Presence did not guarantee the same archive.

This separates Huizinga from Malinowski without making either method pure. Malinowski’s strongest warrant came from living relations that could be revisited, questioned and observed in practice. Huizinga’s comparisons accumulated through travel, reading, memory, cards, reproductions and texts whose makers were often dead. Both could move across distant worlds. What counted as a check on that movement differed.

A 1930 manuscript makes the older vocabulary visible inside Huizinga’s own historical materials. *Trois esprits prégothiques* places *primitive* within twelfth-century intellectual history. A nearby page begins *Cet élément ludique* and develops competition and disputation; a few pages later Huizinga refuses to treat Abelard as a simple precursor of the Renaissance, calling him instead *prégothique*.[^17] Here *primitive* does not name a colonial population or a surviving human stage. It positions a feature within a European historical formation, while the direction of the formation still has to be argued.

The same years produced Huizinga’s intensive work on Philip the Good. His dossier compares portraits and copies, requests museum photographs, distinguishes damaged traditions of testimony, and records new autograph letters that could correct an earlier claim.[^18] None of this constitutes a separate “method.” It shows what had to accumulate between a comparison and a historical conclusion. A question could come from ethnology; the warrant still had to be rebuilt from surviving relations among sources.

Travel therefore did not move the article from abstract categories to unmediated experience. It multiplied mediations. Malinowski’s fieldwork, Huizinga’s American cards, the remembered journey through Java, Bali and Hong Kong, and the photographic and textual reconstruction of Burgundy all made distant worlds portable in different ways. Greater empirical density did not automatically destroy civilizational ordering. It made the route from a label to a conclusion longer and more visible.

That is also why the word *primitive* could travel so easily. It could mark an earlier textual layer, a living ethnographic population, a stage in a course, or a feature inside medieval Europe. Its portability was part of its strength. Its waning lay in the growing amount of work required to specify which of those relations was actually being claimed.

## IV. Leiden, 1931

On 19 July 1931 Huizinga sent Malinowski “a paper which I made up”: a proposal for a Centre of Anthropological Research at Leiden. The paper followed conversations with John Van Sickle and Tracy Kittredge. Huizinga asked Malinowski to judge it and, if he approved, perhaps use his influence with the Rockefeller Foundation. If he disliked it, Huizinga was prepared to let the request disappear.[^19]

The proposal gathered existing strengths. Leiden had an Ethnographical Museum, Oriental languages, the Kern Institute, Sinology and Islamic studies, colonial law and *adat*, and a university long involved in training men for the Indies. It was possible to see these resources as the parts of an anthropological centre before any new discipline had been built around them.

The supporting documents show what happened when comparative vocabulary entered institutional design. Java could be described as “high culture”; other populations as standing at a “backward stage”; Bali as an unusually interesting remnant of Hinduism. A. W. Nieuwenhuis titled his annex “Ethnographical objects as data for psychological research about primitive or semi-cultured races.”[^20] The words did practical classificatory work. Wicker-work, textiles, metalwork and carving could become evidence for memory, reasoning, perseverance or application; judgments about mental capacities could become judgments about further development and, from there, administration or indigenous industry.

This was not the same use of *primitive* that appeared in Huizinga’s twelfth-century notes. The common vocabulary could span a medieval intellectual feature, a living colonial population and a museum object taken as psychological evidence. That portability made the Leiden assemblage imaginable. It also exposed the problem that the word could not solve: what made the assemblage anthropology?

Malinowski was favourable and had already expressed interest to Van Sickle. His approval came with an audit. Who in Leiden could “carry on the work and inspire the institution with their spirit”? What “central aim or principle” could attract strong scholars if the centre lacked first-rate leaders?[^21] Museum buildings and collections were not enough. Languages, law and colonial knowledge were not enough. A field required intellectual direction.

Huizinga replied on 12 August that Malinowski had touched the “weakest spots in our scheme.” Van Vollenhoven’s *adat* work approached anthropological questions, but he was “no anthropologist proper.” Then came the sentence: “I myself am only a benevolent outsider.”[^1] This was not withdrawal. Huizinga had composed the paper, assembled the resources, sent it to Malinowski and sought Rockefeller attention. The outsider had been produced inside the project.

Malinowski’s praise makes the boundary more, not less, precise. He could call Huizinga’s historical method a “really anthropological approach” and still demand anthropological leadership. A question or procedure could cross the line; jurisdiction did not cross automatically with it. The centre converted a methodological ambiguity into a personnel problem.

The problem of *primitive* changed at the same moment. In books and letters the word could remain elastic. In an institution its classifications helped decide which populations, collections and forms of knowledge belonged together and who was qualified to interpret them. The waning of the category was therefore not political disappearance. Its continuing institutional force made the need for explicit warrants more consequential.

The exchange continued after Huizinga named the boundary. In September he sent Malinowski a recently published volume; in October Malinowski thanked him for W. Ahlbrinck’s *Encyclopaedie der Karaïben*, already useful to a pupil working on the couvade. Malinowski also planned to carry Huizinga’s Leiden letter onward in the Rockefeller conversation.[^22] Outsiderhood did not stop circulation. It described the terms under which circulation continued.

## Coda: 1933

Huizinga repeated the distinction in May 1933 when Malinowski stood for the Royal Netherlands Academy. “Being no anthropologist,” he wrote, he had refrained from taking a leading part in the candidature, although he signed it.[^23] The same year he made Malinowski central to his rectoral address *Over de grenzen van spel en ernst in de cultuur*.

The address begins its comparative movement from named practices rather than from a primitive stage. Huizinga praises *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* and describes the Kula through ceremonial circulation, prestige, trust and generosity. He turns to Mauss’s potlatch, where giving is tied to rivalry, rank and return. Then he brings in the 1454 *Vœu du Faisan* of Philip the Good. Burgundian competitive hospitality, he remarks, was “not so very far” from a potlatch.[^23]

The route is bold but not indiscriminate. Kula remains Kula; potlatch remains potlatch; the Burgundian feast remains a historically reconstructed ceremony. Huizinga selects a relation—competitive giving—and moves it across cases whose sources are unlike. The address then changes evidence again, turning to Sanskrit words for play, Semitic vocabulary, Genoa, Antwerp, wagering and calculation. The philologist who had made a “salto mortale” into history has not vanished. Nor has the anthropological primitive. But neither supplies the whole comparison.

This is not yet *Homo Ludens*. Nor is it evidence that *primitive* had been overcome. Huizinga continued to use period terms and stadial language, just as Malinowski continued to write *primitive* and *savage*. What changed was the burden borne by those words. In 1929 “the stage of savage life” could still organize the first division of a course. In 1933 the most striking comparison was carried by circulation, obligation, prestige, rivalry and play.

Huizinga’s own image of waning helps keep the chronology open. In *Herfsttij* old forms do not simply vanish before new ones; they can remain luxuriant, overdeveloped and compelling while the life they organize begins to escape them. Something similar happened to *primitive* in this exchange. The category remained visible precisely while the relations once compressed inside it were being pulled apart and reassigned to ethnography, historical reconstruction, institutional principle and disciplinary judgment.

That process also explains the outsider. *Primitive* had offered a broad corridor between anthropology and history: living peoples could illuminate old forms; old forms could be compared with living practices; law, gift, ritual and mentality could be placed in a common field. Malinowski’s ethnography made the corridor denser. Huizinga’s historical use made its evidentiary asymmetries harder to ignore. Leiden then asked who had authority to organize what moved through it.

“Benevolent outsider” was therefore neither simple modesty nor disciplinary defeat. It named a position created by sustained proximity. Huizinga could enter anthropological questions, carry them into history, send materials back, and help build an institution. What he could not claim automatically was the anthropologist’s jurisdiction over the field. The category travelled farther than disciplinary authority did.

The history is consequently not one of a primitive category yielding to a modern one. It is the waning of a form that remained alive. By 1933 *primitive* still named populations, stages and historical features. It no longer guaranteed, by itself, what any of them meant or how they could be compared. The word remained. More and more of the proof stood outside it.
