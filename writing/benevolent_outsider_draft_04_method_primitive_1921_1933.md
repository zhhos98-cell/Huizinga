# Only a Benevolent Outsider
## Johan Huizinga and Bronisław Malinowski on the Primitive, 1921–1933

In December 1926 Bronisław Malinowski sent Johan Huizinga three books. Huizinga called them “the small, the medium and the big one.” He began with *Crime and Custom in Savage Society*. The joke in his letter came from Milo of Croton, who was said to have trained by carrying a calf until he could carry a bull. Malinowski, Huizinga suggested, meant to accustom him to anthropology in the same way. Then the letter became serious. Malinowski’s “outlook on ethnological problems,” Huizinga wrote, was “just the one I can profit by for my historical method.” He recalled their conversation while walking along the Haarlemsche Trekvaart. Before the month ended he wrote again. “We emphasize the functional side of cultural phenomena.” *Crime and Custom* had led him back to medieval law. Feudal relations came into view. So did troubadour culture. He mentioned the efficacy of words. He also mentioned vanity, generosity and friendship within what he called “so-called primitive culture.”[^1]

The letter is a better beginning than a general account of Huizinga’s friendship with Malinowski. It shows a historian reading anthropology for use in history. It also shows how quickly Huizinga moved away from the Trobriands. He read about law in the western Pacific and thought of feudal Europe. He read about obligation and thought of forms whose force exceeded a written rule. He read about words and thought about what formal speech could do. The comparison began with a question that Huizinga himself called “the functional side of cultural phenomena.” Nothing in the letter suggests that he meant to become an anthropologist. He expected Malinowski’s work to be useful to his “historical method.”

The same letters also place *primitive* at the centre of the exchange. Malinowski’s titles spoke of “savage society.” Huizinga answered with “so-called primitive culture.” In May 1929 he would say that he felt “quite at home in the Trobriands.” The same letter described a Delft course beginning with “the stage of savage life.” Old Aryan culture followed. Rome came after it. In July 1931 a Leiden annex still spoke of “primitive or semi-cultured races.” Malinowski approved much of the plan. He then asked who would “carry on the work and inspire the institution with their spirit.” He also asked for a “central aim or principle.” Huizinga replied that Malinowski had touched the “weakest spots in our scheme.” Van Vollenhoven, he added, was “no anthropologist proper.” Huizinga then wrote: “I myself am only a benevolent outsider.”[^2]

Two years later Huizinga used Malinowski’s Kula in his rectoral address on play and seriousness. He moved from the Kula to Mauss’s potlatch. Then he brought in Philip the Good and the *Vœu du Faisan*. The Burgundian banquet, he remarked, stood “not so very far” from a potlatch. In the same year Malinowski stood for the Royal Netherlands Academy. Huizinga wrote: “Being no anthropologist I refrained from taking a leading part in your candidature, though signing it with some others.”[^3] The 1931 phrase therefore belongs to more than a private exchange. Huizinga used anthropology, supported anthropologists and helped with an anthropological project. He also left the leading part to scholars he regarded as anthropologists proper.

The difference between the two men becomes clearer if their exchange is followed through the words they used. Malinowski wrote of “the functional view of Anthropology.” Huizinga wrote of “historical method,” “social morphology” and “historical use.” Malinowski asked for a “central aim or principle.” Huizinga answered as a “benevolent outsider.” Both men continued to use *primitive* and *savage*. The word remained common while the work around it became more exact. Malinowski’s Trobriand studies made primitive law, primitive exchange and primitive kinship harder to summarize by the adjective alone. Huizinga continued to place forms in historical sequences, yet he repeatedly resisted the idea that a place in a sequence supplied a complete explanation. Their correspondence shows the two practices meeting before either vocabulary disappeared.

The order matters. The argument begins with their exchange because the differences become visible there. It then turns backward. Huizinga met Malinowski after years of writing about development, morphology and historical understanding. Malinowski met Huizinga while presenting anthropology as a science of institutions in relation to one another. The books of 1926 made those earlier positions matter to each other. The letters of 1928 and 1929 show how far the borrowing went. The Leiden papers of 1931 force the question of who counted as an anthropologist. The rectoral address of 1933 shows Huizinga continuing to compare after he had answered that question for himself.

## I. “Just the one I can profit by for my historical method”

Huizinga’s December letters followed several encounters in 1926. Malinowski’s report on his American visit for the Laura Spelman Rockefeller Memorial described “the functional view of Anthropology” and treated anthropology as an integrating social science. He also recorded theoretical discussions at a Harvard seminar attended by R. B. Dixon, Alfred Tozzer, Marcel Mauss and “Professor Huizinga.” Huizinga had crossed the Atlantic in April. His journey took him through universities and social-science institutions under Rockefeller auspices. The Harvard report gives no transcript, though it places Huizinga in a room where Malinowski said these questions were discussed. Huizinga’s December letter supplies another meeting. Their “ethnological problems” had also been discussed while walking along the Haarlemsche Trekvaart.[^4]

The American setting adds another small fact. Malinowski’s report placed Mauss in the Harvard discussion alongside Dixon, Tozzer and Huizinga. The same report was written for the Rockefeller Memorial, whose staff were interested in the organization of social science. Malinowski was therefore speaking about function in a setting where research programmes, university arrangements and public uses of social science were being compared. Huizinga’s later request about Leiden followed an earlier encounter with these institutional questions. In 1926 he had already encountered Malinowski while these questions were part of the visit.

Malinowski’s American report matters because its language returns in 1931. In 1926 he was already interested in how social science could be organized and how it entered public life. He described anthropology through the work of institutions within an “integral” cultural scheme. He also treated anthropology as capable of bringing several kinds of social study together. Five years later, when Huizinga sent him the Leiden proposal, Malinowski again asked for a principle that could make a collection of resources into anthropology. The wording changed with the occasion. The question remained recognizable.

Huizinga had set out his own position shortly before sailing. On 7 April 1926 he addressed the Historisch Genootschap in Utrecht on the task and terminology of cultural history. One thesis attacked *evolutie* as a general historical principle and said that it had done particular damage in the history of religion and art. Another described cultural history as social morphology rather than social psychology. Huizinga’s object was what connected people. He also wrote of forms of life and thought, then of the structure of society. A further thesis named ethnology among the special sciences that had long practised such morphology.[^5] The theses preceded Huizinga’s departure by about a week. Huizinga already had morphology, a criticism of general evolution and an explicit place for ethnology before meeting Malinowski.

The 1929 expansion of the Utrecht address makes the place of ethnology still clearer in Huizinga’s own wording. Religious studies and ethnology could define recurrent forms such as myth, consecration, sacred action, combat-play and secret society. Cultural history could then ask how such forms appeared in particular historical circumstances and how their meaning changed. Huizinga also wrote that cultural history should learn from sociology. He named the “practical sociology” of Americans and the work of Marcel Mauss. The contact was desirable because the subjects met in practice. The historian still followed forms through historical change rather than handing the work over to sociology.[^11]

This distinction also helps with the word *morphology*. Huizinga used it for social forms rather than for a fixed sequence of stages. In the Utrecht theses the word referred to forms through which social life became visible. Ethnology had already studied such forms. Art history and literary history had done the same in their own materials. The danger he associated with *evolutie* was different. A general evolutionary principle could turn the order of development into the explanation. His 1921 criticism of Wells and his 1926 theses meet at this point without requiring identical language.

The Utrecht formulation also gives a more exact setting for the December phrase “functional side of cultural phenomena.” Huizinga had already defined cultural history through forms and social connections. What he found in Malinowski was a way of pursuing such questions in materials that seemed far removed from his medieval work. *Crime and Custom* examined the force of law where codified statute seemed an inadequate model. Malinowski followed obligation into reciprocity, kin relations and public pressure. The Trobriand case showed rules working through social relations that could be observed in practice. Huizinga saw an immediate use for history. Medieval law also contained obligations whose force exceeded a formal rule.

The comparison becomes more precise in Huizinga’s letter of 31 December. Feudalism appears beside Malinowski. Troubadour culture appears too. Huizinga turns to the efficacy of words and to the difference between formal statements and emotional usage. He names vanity, generosity and friendship as familiar motives in “so-called primitive culture.” The important words are his. He says “functional side.” He says “my approach to history.” He says “so-called primitive culture.” The letter stays with examples rather than a theory of combining anthropology and history. It records Huizinga trying Malinowski’s questions on materials he already knew.

The road to that moment goes farther back. In 1921 Huizinga reviewed H. G. Wells and Oswald Spengler together in *Twee worstelaars met den engel*. Wells’s *Outline of History* led from natural development into human history on a vast scale. Huizinga separated biological or geological development from “the function of historical understanding.” Events in both cases happened in time. That common chronology still left distinct forms of knowledge. History, in Huizinga’s account, concerned human relations and thoughts that could be understood as human experience.[^6]

Religion gave him a sharper example. Wells preferred a line that led toward moral universalism and useful labour. Huizinga thought the line left too much of religious history behind. Cult belonged to the historical world. Sacrament belonged there too. Symbol and mysticism followed. Their historical place remained part of the past even when they led nowhere toward Wells’s preferred future. Huizinga’s objection was practical. A history of religion that selected what contributed to progress failed to describe the religious life of earlier periods.

Spengler pressed in another direction. Huizinga took morphology seriously and placed the distinction between historical and natural-scientific concept formation within the work of Dilthey, Windelband, Rickert and Simmel. He therefore denied Spengler the originality Spengler claimed. He also tested the *Morphologie der Weltgeschichte* against historical particulars. A morphological comparison could be useful. A system that decided the meaning of each particular in advance distorted the material. Huizinga’s criticism of Wells and Spengler therefore left him with large comparison and morphology, but with limits on what sequence or form could decide before historical work began.

Marc Bloch supplied a different case in 1925. Huizinga reviewed *Les rois thaumaturges* and followed Bloch’s treatment of royal healing in France and England. The review takes seriously the persistence of belief even when cures failed. It follows the relation between sacral kingship, anointing and political legitimacy. Huizinga’s criticism falls heavily on the arrangement of the mass of evidence and on the length of the demonstration.[^7] Bloch therefore belongs in the background as a brief comparison. Ritual efficacy and sacral power were already legitimate historical questions for Huizinga. Malinowski’s importance lay elsewhere.

*Crime and Custom* placed such questions inside a living society and described the relations through which they worked. Malinowski argued against a picture of savage life ruled simply by blind custom. Trobriand obligations were distributed through reciprocal services. Rights and duties followed kin relations. Exchange created expectations. Public knowledge mattered. The breach of a rule had consequences because other relations were attached to it. “Primitive law” remained the title of the problem, while the explanation moved into particulars of social life.

Huizinga could take this directly into medieval history. Feudal relations depended on recognition and rank. Public ceremony mattered. A vow could bind because it was made under recognized conditions. Generosity could be represented as virtue and still become expected conduct. Troubadour poetry gave social form to relations of service and honour. Huizinga kept Burgundy and feudal Europe distinct from the Trobriands. He could ask what made a form effective after formal definition had failed to answer the question.

Words offered a second point of contact. Malinowski had already made language a field in which use mattered. Huizinga’s December letter also turned to the force of words. Medieval actors lived among formulas. A word could promise. It could insult. It could establish a relation. The force depended on the speaker and the audience. Convention fixed the setting. Huizinga’s own phrase “functional side” fits these cases closely. The letter moves from law to words because both raised the same practical difficulty: the form had to be seen in use.

The difference between their materials was also clear from the start. Malinowski could observe conduct and compare statements with practice. He could hear a word in more than one situation. He could follow exchange among partners. Huizinga’s medieval actors were dead. Their actions survived in chronicles and legal texts. Literature preserved other traces. Images survived too, sometimes through later copies. A historian could ask what a vow or ceremony did, but the answer depended on what had survived and how it had reached him. The difference appears in the work each man had to do before writing.

Huizinga’s research on older India shows that *primitive* already had several uses in his own work. Notes on Indian medicine distinguished earlier layers from later analytical or systematic formulations and used *primitief* or *primitieve* inside that discussion. Elsewhere headings such as *Primitivisme* appear among his working papers. These materials belong to philological and historical work rather than to a Malinowski file. Their value is simple. Huizinga had used the word before the friendship. In his hands it could identify an earlier layer within a textual or intellectual history.

That earlier usage matters when he writes “so-called primitive culture” to Malinowski. The qualification is small, though it leaves the phrase less secure than “primitive culture” used as a self-explanatory term. Huizinga recognizes vanity, generosity and friendship across the comparison. The shared motives make the Trobriand material familiar. The adjective does less work than the examples around it.

The first year of their exchange therefore produces a compact sequence. Malinowski discusses the “functional view of Anthropology” in America. Huizinga arrives with his own criticism of general evolution and his own “social morphology.” Their conversations continue in the Netherlands. Malinowski sends three books. Huizinga begins with *Crime and Custom*. Within weeks he says the “outlook on ethnological problems” can profit his historical method and that the two scholars emphasize the “functional side of cultural phenomena.” The words belong to them. The argument lies in the sequence.

## II. “Quite at home in the Trobriands”

By 1928 Huizinga was reading Malinowski’s newer work and asking Malinowski to read his own. On 29 March he wrote after *Sex and Repression in Savage Society*. The book had given him “a great deal of new visions concerning the very origins of culture,” especially in its final part. Yet he thought *Crime and Custom* had perhaps been even more useful “for historical use.”[^8] Huizinga distinguished “origins of culture” from “historical use” in the same letter. The first invited large questions. The second meant bringing Malinowski into problems that a historian could actually work on.

Huizinga’s phrase “for historical use” deserves to be kept beside the praise of origins. It appears after two years of reading Malinowski and after their conversations in America and the Netherlands. He ranked the books by what they gave his work. *Sex and Repression* opened “the very origins of culture.” *Crime and Custom* remained especially useful when he returned to law, custom and historical societies. The distinction is small but persistent throughout the correspondence: large anthropological questions interest him, while the strongest praise often arrives when he can carry a question back into history.

The letter also contained disagreement. *Sex and Repression* had reorganized a Freudian question through differences in family structure. Huizinga resisted the reasoning. A historian, he wrote, was relatively proof against it because he knew “the immense complexity of the smallest social phenomenon.” That sentence matters because it gives his objection in his own language. Psychology remains within reach; his objection concerns the reasoning in this case. He objects to a line of reasoning that appears too simple for the complexity he expects even in a small social phenomenon.

The next sentences complicate any flattering reading of the remark. Huizinga characterized Freud and Marx through antisemitic language about a “Talmudic mind.” The same letter that invokes the “immense complexity” of social phenomena also produces a coarse cultural stereotype. The point deserves to remain visible. Huizinga resisted one explanation and generalized crudely in the next sentences. The letter shows a historian selecting among explanations, sometimes carefully and sometimes badly.

Huizinga then sent his own remarks on American culture to Malinowski and asked for criticism. Malinowski replied on 15 August. “We often speak of you,” he wrote. “The reading of your American notes was a most stimulating event (we read them aloud).”[^9] The detail is brief and useful. Huizinga was no longer only receiving anthropology. His writing had become material for Malinowski’s judgment. The manuscript had also entered a domestic setting, where it was read aloud.

The surviving correspondence also shows reading continuing through conversation. Huizinga and Malinowski referred back to earlier meetings. Their encounters belonged to travel between institutions rather than to a stable shared department. A seminar in America supplied one occasion. A walk near Leiden supplied another. Letters kept the discussion going. The informality helped the exchange because neither man had to state a complete programme each time. A phrase such as “functional side” could be enough when both remembered earlier discussion.

Huizinga’s surviving American materials help identify the kind of work behind those remarks. Turner appears in the files. Helen Hunt Jackson appears. James Mooney’s work on the Ghost Dance appears among cards on treaties, reservations and Indian Territory. Several of these slips are followed by sustained comparative prose on America and Europe. The exact packet Malinowski heard remains unidentified in the surviving papers. They do show Huizinga moving from travel to reading, from notes to comparison. The names can remain quick. Their role is to keep Huizinga’s “American notes” attached to an actual desk full of sources.

In May 1929 the exchange gives the article one of its most useful sentences. Huizinga had received *The Sexual Life of Savages in North-Western Melanesia*. After Malinowski’s earlier books, he wrote, he felt “quite at home in the Trobriands.” Then he described what he had done with those books during the previous winter. At the Delft Technical University he had given a course organized around *homo faber*. It began with “the stage of savage life.” From there it moved to old Aryan culture, then to the Roman Empire and its decay. Huizinga told Malinowski that he had “profited immensely” from his work.[^10]

The letter should be allowed to stand with little protection around it. Trobriand life had become familiar through Malinowski. Huizinga still called the first part of his course “the stage of savage life.” He still moved from that stage to old Aryan culture and Rome. Huizinga placed Malinowski’s books inside his own sequence. At the same time, “quite at home in the Trobriands” tells us that the first position in the sequence had become far more specific than the phrase “savage life” suggests.

This is where *primitive* becomes especially useful for comparison. Malinowski had kept the older words in titles and prose. His field descriptions gave them less power to settle the matter by themselves. “Savage law” could no longer mean absence of binding rules once *Crime and Custom* had reconstructed obligations in detail. A Trobriand exchange could no longer be dismissed as simple barter once relations of prestige and reciprocity were followed. Kinship became a set of rights and duties traced through practice rather than a bare scheme. The word still located a recognized field of study. The work had to be done elsewhere, in the relations Malinowski described.

Huizinga kept “stage” because it still served his course. His 1921 criticism of Wells had already shown that he could use sequence while objecting to what sequence was made to prove. The Delft letter gives a concrete version. “Savage life,” old Aryan culture and Rome are placed one after another. Nothing in the surviving letter says that a law, motive or ceremony in one stage can therefore be explained by its place in the sequence. The work of explanation appears in the books from which Huizinga says he “profited immensely.”

The public essay *De taak der cultuurgeschiedenis* appeared in 1929 and expanded the Utrecht theses. Huizinga wrote that cultural history had to remain in contact with neighbouring fields. He mentioned legal history and economic history. Religious history followed. Ethnology and sociology stood close by. Boundaries were crossed repeatedly. They still retained significance. He could point to the practical sociology of Americans and to Marcel Mauss while insisting on a task for cultural history itself.[^11] The essay therefore belongs close to the 1929 letter. Huizinga’s private use of Malinowski sat beside a public account in which crossing into ethnology or sociology was normal work.

His lectures on seventeenth-century Dutch civilization around 1930 gave the same position a concrete form. Geography mattered to the rise of Dutch culture. So did social structure. Economic conditions entered the account. Political arrangements entered it too. Huizinga could even mention ethnographic conditions. Yet an enumeration of such conditions was only a preliminary description of Dutch civilization.[^12] He moved from conditions toward the particular forms in which the civilization appeared.

The Dutch-culture lectures sharpen the point with a sentence close to the subject of this article. Huizinga could list social, economic and political conditions and add, “if one wishes,” ethnographic conditions. He then said that such an enumeration could never completely explain a civilization. The conditions remained necessary for the preliminary description of country and society. His account of culture continued beyond them. This is the same Huizinga who was reading Malinowski during these years. He had room for ethnographic conditions inside cultural history without making them the final account.

Here Huizinga’s language remains useful. He spoke of *cultuurgeschiedenis*. He spoke of forms and of social morphology. He kept these neighbouring sciences beside cultural history. Malinowski’s American report had been more ambitious about anthropology as an integrating science. The 1931 Leiden exchange would make that difference practical.

Before that happened, a 1930 manuscript shows Huizinga using *primitive* inside European intellectual history. *Trois esprits prégothiques* concerned the twelfth century. One page begins with *Cet élément ludique* and develops competition and disputation. The next page uses *primitive* within the same argument. A few pages later Huizinga refuses to make Abelard a precursor of the Renaissance. He describes him instead as *prégothique* and places the apparent novelty of his thought within another intellectual formation.[^13]

The example matters because “primitive” here describes neither a colonial population nor a surviving people placed at the beginning of a human sequence. It appears within the European Middle Ages. The word helps Huizinga describe an intellectual apparatus that looks earlier or less differentiated in some respects. Abelard remains *prégothique* rather than the first step toward a later Renaissance. Huizinga can call a feature *primitive* and still refuse a simple precursor story.

This is close to what his 1921 criticism of Spengler required. Large forms were useful if the particulars retained their own historical place. In 1930 Huizinga could identify a *primitive* element and a *ludique* element while keeping the twelfth century distinct from the Renaissance. The word helped position a feature. The direction of the whole period still required historical argument.

The same years also produced Huizinga’s intensive work on Philip the Good. His portrait dossier is useful because it shows the historian at work on evidence while he was reading Malinowski. Huizinga compared several portraits and copies. He rejected the easy move of reading character directly from a face. Written testimony formed one damaged tradition. Images formed another. Copies could still matter if their relation to earlier images could be established. Museum photographs allowed the comparison of objects held in different places. Newly recovered autograph letters could correct an earlier assertion about Philip’s character.[^14]

The Philip papers make this point unusually tangible. Huizinga wrote to Gotha in November 1929 for a good photograph of Philip, specifying an approximate size of twenty by twenty-six centimetres. Other papers record photographic reproduction, proofs and copies. He compared portraits held in different places and considered whether later images could descend from an earlier lost model. The dossier also preserves the arrival of autograph letters used to correct an earlier statement about Philip’s character. Historical comparison here depends on very ordinary acts. Huizinga locates an image and obtains a reproduction. He places copies beside one another. A new document can make him change a sentence.

The dossier explains why “historical use” had a particular meaning for Huizinga. A question could come from elsewhere. The historian still had to decide which sources could answer it. A copy might preserve an image lost in its original form. A later photograph could make comparison possible. A new letter could force a correction. None of this resembles the situation of Malinowski in the Trobriands, where statements could be checked against living conduct. Huizinga’s materials arrived through survival and transmission.

That difference appears again when comparison becomes explicit. Feudal obligation could be placed beside Trobriand reciprocity. The medieval case still depended on its own texts and ceremonies. Huizinga still had to reconstruct what feudal obligations meant in those texts and ceremonies. Ceremonial giving could be compared across periods. The documents of a Burgundian court still had their own dates, conventions and problems of survival. Malinowski’s question could move. The historian’s evidence remained historical.

The 1928 and 1929 letters therefore show more than reciprocal friendship. Huizinga praised Malinowski’s books and objected to one line of reasoning. He sent his own work for judgment. He also used Trobriand material in teaching. Malinowski read Huizinga aloud. The term *primitive* stayed with both men. So did large comparison. What changed was the amount of material that had to stand between a label and a conclusion.

## III. “The weakest spots in our scheme”

On 19 July 1931 Huizinga sent Malinowski another kind of text. “I am sending you a paper which I made up,” he began. The paper followed conversations with John Van Sickle and Tracy Kittredge, who had come to Leiden “to look into the matter.” Huizinga asked Malinowski to read the proposal for a Centre of Anthropological Research. If Malinowski approved, he might use his influence with the Rockefeller Foundation. He might be asked for an opinion, or he might “drop in a word unasked.” If he disliked the proposal, Huizinga was ready to let the request disappear.[^15]

The memorandum treated the reorganization of Leiden’s Ethnographical Museum as an opportunity. Leiden already possessed collections and institutes that could be brought closer together. Oriental languages were part of the proposal. The Kern Institute supplied Indological scholarship. Sinology and Islamic studies added other regions. Colonial law and *adat* studies were close at hand. The university also trained men for work in the Indies. Huizinga’s paper gathered these existing resources and described what might be built from them.

The supporting documents also show the language in which peoples and materials were arranged. Java could appear as “high culture.” Other populations could be placed at a “backward stage.” Bali appeared as an unusually interesting remnant of Hinduism. A. W. Nieuwenhuis made the connection to *primitive* explicit in the title of his annex: “Ethnographical objects as data for psychological research about primitive or semi-cultured races.”[^16]

Nieuwenhuis proposed to read ethnographical objects for mental capacities. Wicker-work and textiles could reveal forms of memory or reasoning. Metalwork and carving could indicate application or perseverance. Such judgments could then bear on prospects for further development. From there the argument could reach questions of administration or indigenous industry. The chain is present in the document itself. An object became evidence about mind. The judgment about mind became a judgment about a population’s future.

This usage of *primitive* differs sharply from Huizinga’s *primitive* in *Trois esprits prégothiques*. Nieuwenhuis was writing about living peoples in a colonial setting. His title joined ethnographical objects to psychological research and then to “primitive or semi-cultured races.” Huizinga’s French manuscript used the same broad vocabulary inside twelfth-century intellectual history. The common word carried different uses.

Malinowski replied on 30 July from Soprabolzano. He was favourable to the Leiden idea and had already expressed strong interest to Van Sickle. His approval came with two questions. Who in Leiden could “carry on the work and inspire the institution with their spirit”? What “central aim or principle” could make the centre attractive to strong scholars if Leiden lacked first-rate leaders?[^17] These were practical questions about the centre as an anthropological institution.

Malinowski’s questions also returned to the position he had stated during the American trip. For Malinowski, relevant materials still required a scientific principle and leaders. Museum objects, languages and legal expertise could all be valuable. The centre still required a principle and people capable of carrying on the work. His criticism concerned what the proposal lacked. He regarded much of its contents as valuable.

The memorandum’s own breadth helps explain Malinowski’s unease. It was possible to point to a museum, specialists in Oriental languages, *adat* law and institutes concerned with Asia and call the combination a promising centre. Malinowski wanted to know who would give it intellectual direction. His letter asked instead about the people already there and the principle under which their work could meet. Huizinga’s admission about the “weakest spots” answers exactly those questions.

Huizinga replied on 12 August. Malinowski, he admitted, had touched the “weakest spots in our scheme.” The personnel problem came first. Van Vollenhoven’s work on *adat* law came very close to anthropological questions, but Huizinga called him “no anthropologist proper.” Then Huizinga described himself: “I myself am only a benevolent outsider.”[^18] The phrase belongs to this concrete question of leadership. Huizinga had made the paper. He had brought institutions together. He had sent the plan to Malinowski. He could still say that he was outside the discipline whose centre they were discussing.

Malinowski’s praise makes the distinction sharper. He could recognize what he called a “really anthropological approach” in Huizinga’s work and still insist on leadership by anthropologists. The letters therefore separate usefulness from professional standing in language supplied by the two men themselves. Huizinga could “profit” from anthropology. Malinowski could call an approach “really anthropological.” Van Vollenhoven could come close to anthropology. Yet when Huizinga asked who should lead a centre, “anthropologist proper” became decisive.

The memorandum also brought the problem of *primitive* into an institution. Leiden’s collections had been formed through older ethnology and colonial knowledge. Oriental scholarship gave language and textual access. *Adat* law linked scholarship to colonial administration. Nieuwenhuis’s annex treated objects as evidence for “primitive or semi-cultured races.” Malinowski accepted these resources and asked what would give them a “central aim or principle.”

Huizinga’s own lectures on Dutch civilization help explain why his memorandum began by gathering existing fields. In cultural history he was accustomed to bringing several kinds of knowledge into one account. Geography could be relevant without becoming the whole explanation. Economics could be relevant without taking over the field. Political history could enter. Ethnographic conditions could enter too. The historian’s task remained to describe the culture whose forms changed through time.

Malinowski wanted more unity from anthropology. His American report had already presented anthropology as an integrating social science. His Leiden letter asked for a centre that could attract people through a strong scientific aim. Huizinga’s “benevolent outsider” was therefore a direct answer to a stronger claim about the discipline. He could help assemble a project. He left the standing of “anthropologist proper” to others.

The correspondence continued immediately. On 15 September Huizinga sent a recently published volume. On 4 October Malinowski thanked him for W. Ahlbrinck’s *Encyclopaedie der Karaïben*. A pupil staying with him and working on the couvade was already using it. Malinowski also sent Huizinga a Bertrand Russell review. He planned to take Huizinga’s 12 August letter to Paris, where Van Sickle might raise the Leiden question.[^19]

The 4 October letter also adds a small example of how easily materials continued to cross the line Huizinga had just drawn. Malinowski thanked him for the *Encyclopaedie der Karaïben* because it was already useful to a pupil working on the couvade. The book concerned Carib language, customs and manners. Huizinga had sent a newly published volume; Malinowski put it to work in research. The exchange required no agreement that Huizinga himself was an anthropologist. It required access to a useful book and a reader who knew who could use it.

The sequence is useful precisely because no theoretical reconciliation follows the August letter. Huizinga calls himself an outsider, then sends material useful to anthropological research. Malinowski passes the book to a pupil and carries the Leiden letter toward a Rockefeller conversation. The phrase “benevolent outsider” leaves their work together intact.

The Leiden episode also shows why the history cannot be reduced to friendship. Friendship explains why Huizinga could ask Malinowski for help and why Malinowski answered at length. The important content lies in the questions that became unavoidable once an institute required leaders. “Who will carry on the work?” “What central aim or principle?” “No anthropologist proper.” “Only a benevolent outsider.” These phrases give the episode its structure.

## IV. “Being no anthropologist”

Huizinga repeated the same position in May 1933. Malinowski was a candidate for the Royal Netherlands Academy. Huizinga wrote: “Being no anthropologist I refrained from taking a leading part in your candidature, though signing it with some others.”[^20] The wording closely matches the action he had described in 1931. He took part. He signed. He left the leading part to others because the candidature concerned anthropology.

The same year he used Malinowski publicly in *Over de grenzen van spel en ernst in de cultuur*. The address moves quickly. Huizinga tells his audience that anyone who has read Malinowski’s “brilliant and fascinating” *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* will know the Kula. He describes the circulation of valued objects and the honour attached to the exchange. Direct utility explains little. Prestige, trust and generosity matter. Malinowski’s account, Huizinga says, illuminated similar phenomena elsewhere and added a new chapter to ethnology.[^21]

Huizinga’s treatment of the Kula follows Malinowski closely enough to make the borrowing visible. He stresses that the objects circulate ceremonially and that ordinary economic utility is secondary. Honour and prestige attach to the movement. Trust matters between partners. Generosity matters too. Huizinga then says that Malinowski’s description illuminated related phenomena in other places. The praise concerns what the ethnography made visible in the Kula itself.

The Kula takes Huizinga back to the problem first raised by *Crime and Custom*. A form that looks strange from outside acquires force once the relations around it are described. The valuable object is part of a circuit. Its movement matters. The partners matter. Honour matters. The object cannot be understood through utility alone. The broad word *primitive* adds little to this part of the argument.

He then turns to Mauss and the potlatch. Giving is connected to rivalry and rank. The ceremonial act creates obligations of return. Huizinga asks whether this kind of display belongs to play. Then he moves to the Burgundian court. At Lille in 1454 the *Vœu du Faisan* involved a sequence of banquets in which luxury and hospitality passed competitively from one participant to another. Huizinga remarks that this was “not so very far” from a potlatch.

The potlatch passage makes the route of comparison even clearer. Huizinga uses Mauss for the ceremonial gift and the obligation of return. Competitive display brings the case toward play. Philip then enters through a courtly feast whose extravagance moved from host to host. The sequence is quick. Huizinga compares competitive giving in Kwakiutl society with competitive giving in Burgundy. He selects the part that matters to his lecture, the competitive gift, and moves on.

Philip the Good had occupied Huizinga’s desk for years. The comparison brought a heavily researched medieval example into the same inquiry. Huizinga knew the Burgundian case through a large body of historical material. His earlier dossier on Philip had forced him to compare portraits with later copies. Museum photographs entered the comparison. So did autograph letters. In 1933 he placed a Burgundian ceremony beside ethnographic exchange because competitive giving supplied a point of comparison.

The cases keep their own names. Kula remains Kula. Potlatch remains potlatch. The *Vœu du Faisan* remains a Burgundian vow and banquet. Huizinga keeps all three under their own names. He moves rapidly from one to another and states the resemblance he wants. The phrase “not so very far” opens comparison while keeping the cases separate.

The address then changes materials again. Huizinga turns to Sanskrit words for play. Semitic vocabulary enters through information from A. J. Wensinck. Philology, the field in which Huizinga had been trained, returns inside the same address. The question remains play and seriousness. The evidence changes.

Economic history follows. Genoa and Antwerp appear. Wagering and calculation approach one another. Huizinga considers forms related to futures and insurance. Again the examples pass quickly. The point is visible in the movement of the lecture itself. Kula, potlatch, Philip, Sanskrit, Semitic languages, Genoa and Antwerp are brought into the same inquiry without becoming the same kind of source.

This is the best place to see what “benevolent outsider” allowed in practice. Huizinga had just declined a leading role in Malinowski’s Academy candidature because he was “no anthropologist.” He could still make Malinowski central to a public lecture. He could cite *Argonauts* with admiration. He could take the Kula as a case strong enough to illuminate European material.

The old word *primitive* has a smaller role here than the specified practices. Kula is described through ceremonial circulation. Potlatch is described through giving and rivalry. The Burgundian banquet is described through competitive hospitality. He states a resemblance in practice rather than identity through a primitive stage.

This differs from the Delft course of 1928–29. There “the stage of savage life” supplied the first large division. By 1933 Huizinga could still use such language elsewhere, yet his most striking comparison in the rectoral address proceeds differently. It selects a form of action and follows it across cases.

Older categories continued throughout these years. Huizinga continued to use period terms. He continued to speak of earlier and later formations. His 1930 *Trois esprits* had used *primitive* in the Middle Ages. What changes is the burden placed on the word in each argument. A stage can place a case. The case itself still has to be described.

Malinowski’s work had pushed in the same direction from the ethnographic side. *Crime and Custom* made “primitive law” a problem rather than an answer. *Argonauts* made exchange intelligible through the Kula itself. *Sexual Life* and *Sex and Repression* made kinship and family relations objects of sustained description. Malinowski kept “primitive” in his vocabulary while hundreds of pages supplied what the adjective hid. Law received a full account. Exchange did too. Kinship and language followed.

Huizinga’s historical work added another demand. An analogy had to pass through historical evidence. Philip’s ceremony came through Burgundian records rather than observation. The portrait dossier had already shown Huizinga correcting earlier claims when new documents appeared. A medieval comparison could be bold because the source criticism remained separate from the analogy.

This helps explain the unusual ease of the 1933 lecture. Huizinga moves between fields with little pause. Malinowski appears, then Mauss, then Philip. Wensinck enters when the language changes. The argument can move quickly because each name supplies a limited piece. Malinowski supplies the Kula. Mauss supplies the potlatch. Philip supplies a Burgundian ceremony. Wensinck supplies Semitic language material. No one name supplies the whole method.

The result also clarifies the 1926 phrase “the functional side of cultural phenomena.” Huizinga had first used Malinowski to think about law and words. Generosity and friendship followed in the same letter. In 1933 he again asks what ceremonial giving does. The continuity lies in the question. The sources and periods multiply.

The difference between the two men remained visible at the same time. Malinowski’s strongest warrant came from fieldwork and the study of living institutions. Huizinga’s strongest historical claims came from texts, images and surviving objects. His Philip dossier had taught him to compare traditions and copies. The two kinds of evidence could meet in one comparison without becoming interchangeable.

This is why the 1933 Academy letter belongs beside the rectoral address. “Being no anthropologist” sits beside the enthusiastic use of *Argonauts*. Together they state Huizinga’s position more exactly than a general statement that the two disciplines had become one. He could use anthropology while leaving the leading part in anthropology to anthropologists.

## Conclusion

The period from 1921 to 1933 can be followed through a series of specific texts. In 1921 Huizinga separated biological development from historical understanding and criticized Wells for selecting religious history according to a preferred line of progress. He also criticized Spengler when morphology began to determine the meaning of particulars in advance. In April 1926 he attacked *evolutie* as a general principle of cultural history and described cultural history as social morphology. Ethnology already stood among the neighbouring sciences.

Then Malinowski entered the story in person. The Harvard seminar and the Haarlemsche Trekvaart conversation preceded the books. In December 1926 Huizinga said Malinowski’s “outlook on ethnological problems” was “just the one I can profit by for my historical method.” By the end of the month he wrote, “We emphasize the functional side of cultural phenomena.” Medieval law followed immediately. So did feudal relations and words.

In 1928 Huizinga praised *Sex and Repression* for new views on “the very origins of culture.” He still preferred *Crime and Custom* for “historical use.” Against one Freudian line of reasoning he invoked “the immense complexity of the smallest social phenomenon.” Malinowski later read Huizinga’s American notes aloud.

The 1929 Delft letter brings the central difficulty onto one page. Huizinga felt “quite at home in the Trobriands.” He had also taught a course beginning with “the stage of savage life.” He had “profited immensely” from Malinowski. The older sequence remained while Malinowski’s books gave its first term far more content than the phrase itself contained.

Huizinga’s own work during these years gives the comparison another side. *De taak der cultuurgeschiedenis* expected contact with ethnology and sociology while retaining a task for cultural history. *Trois esprits prégothiques* used *primitive* inside twelfth-century Europe and refused to make Abelard a simple Renaissance precursor. The Philip dossier shows how much work could be required before a historical comparison was secure. Copies had to be related. New letters could correct old judgments.

The Leiden proposal of 1931 brought these matters into a practical decision. Nieuwenhuis spoke of “primitive or semi-cultured races.” Malinowski asked who would lead the work and what “central aim or principle” would make the proposed centre strong. Huizinga admitted the “weakest spots in our scheme.” Van Vollenhoven was “no anthropologist proper.” Huizinga was “only a benevolent outsider.”

The phrase lasted. In 1933 he again wrote, “Being no anthropologist I refrained from taking a leading part,” though he signed Malinowski’s candidature. That same year he praised *Argonauts* and used the Kula. Potlatch came next. Then came the *Vœu du Faisan*. The comparison worked through ceremonial giving rather than a shared civilizational stage.

Across these texts *primitive* remains visible, but its use changes with the case. Sometimes it marks a stage. Sometimes it marks an earlier intellectual form. Nieuwenhuis applies it to living peoples. Malinowski uses it while describing institutions whose complexity cannot be read from the label. Huizinga uses it while continuing to distinguish periods and sources. The word remains. More of the argument is carried by the description that follows it.

This is the point at which the relation between Huizinga and Malinowski becomes historically useful. Their friendship kept the exchange open. The correspondence shows the exchange. The Delft course shows Malinowski inside Huizinga’s teaching. The Leiden memorandum shows the question moving into an institution. The rectoral address shows it in public. Malinowski’s work gave Huizinga new material for “historical use.” Huizinga kept asking what his own sources could sustain. The two men could agree on the “functional side of cultural phenomena” and still divide the labour differently when an anthropological institute required leaders.

“Only a benevolent outsider” is therefore best read in the setting in which Huizinga wrote it. It followed Malinowski’s questions about personnel and a “central aim or principle.” It was repeated in action when Huizinga declined the leading part in the Academy candidature. He still used the Kula in his own work. The phrase marks the point at which use, support and leadership ceased to be the same thing.

The history of *primitive* in their exchange is equally concrete. A Trobriand institution could enter medieval comparison. “Savage life” could still head a Delft course. A twelfth-century intellectual form could be called *primitive*. A Leiden annex could speak of “primitive or semi-cultured races.” By 1933 Huizinga could compare Kula, potlatch and Burgundy through ceremonial giving. The sequence shows the word remaining while the cases around it demanded more exact description.
