# Only a Benevolent Outsider
## Johan Huizinga, Bronisław Malinowski, and the Waning of the Primitive, 1926–1933

By August 1931, five years after their American encounter, Johan Huizinga had read Bronisław Malinowski for years, exchanged work with him, and helped frame a proposal for a Centre of Anthropological Research at Leiden. Asked who could give the centre a ‘central aim or principle’, he answered: ‘I myself am only a benevolent outsider.’[^1]

The phrase is harder to place than it looks. Huizinga had used Malinowski in teaching and medieval history; Malinowski had read Huizinga and praised his historical approach; Huizinga was now assembling an anthropological project. Why did proximity produce an outsider?

*Primitive* matters here because it was one of the categories through which that proximity became possible and difficult. Malinowski wrote of savage and primitive societies; Huizinga of ‘so-called primitive culture’, a ‘stage of savage life’, and *primitief* in historical and philological settings. Their exchange did not produce one definition. It repeatedly shifted attention from what a people or culture could be called to what relations observed there could disclose elsewhere: reciprocity, obligation, knowledge, efficacy, rivalry, prestige, historical return. The article therefore follows an exchange and its comparative work, using *primitive* as a pressure test for what could travel between anthropology and history and what still had to be proved again.

In the modern human sciences, *primitive* had acquired a particularly consequential historical use. Difference visible in the present could be made to stand for anteriority in human history: a living people, custom or object could be classified as primitive and thereby positioned nearer an earlier phase of human development (Fabian, 1983; Banerjee, 2006; Pels, 2022). Classification could therefore carry a temporal inference with it. By the interwar period the vocabulary remained widespread, while increasingly dense descriptions of law, mentality, exchange and culture placed more steps between the label and the conclusion.

This article deliberately borrows Huizinga’s own language of waning as a second-order historical term. The borrowing comes from the self-reflexive cautions in the 1919 preface to *Herfsttij*. Huizinga warned that attention fixed on decline could cast ‘te veel van de schaduw des doods’ over the work, and ended by asking whether historical inquiry could approach ‘den wezenlijken inhoud ... die in die vormen heeft gerust’ (Huizinga, 1919). He had opened the same preface by criticizing the habit of viewing each period above all for the promises it held for the next. *Waning* here therefore does not mean disappearance. It names a form that remains available while less of the explanatory work can be left inside it.

Writing *Mijn weg tot de historie* in 1943, Huizinga offered a retrospective control. He remembered reading and excerpting Max Müller as unquestionable wisdom before Tylor’s *Primitive Culture* opened vistas that remained ‘kiemen van mijn wetenschappelijk denken’, even though its animist thesis had long become obsolete and been abandoned (Huizinga, 1947). His own intellectual genealogy thus ran through the nineteenth-century comparative world in which origins, living difference and cultural sequence had been drawn together. Malinowski likewise did not enter their encounter from outside that inheritance. Recent work has weakened the familiar story of his sudden passage from evolutionism to functionalism, describing a refocusing towards intensive local study rather than a disciplinary reset (Lagerspetz and Suolinna, 2025).

That makes their relation more useful than a simple influence story. Most accounts explain how anthropology reworked its own *primitive*; they say less about what happened when ethnographic questions entered another historical craft whose sources, comparisons and claims to authority were organized differently. Recent Huizinga scholarship has meanwhile insisted on putting his historical work back inside the pressures of his own present (Rydin, 2024), while intellectual history has moved from origins and influence toward reception, circulation and active reuse (Wickberg, 2023; Morgan, 2025). The pair archive makes those choices visible in books read and returned, comparisons attempted, journeys remembered, institutional programmes audited, and transfers deliberately left incomplete.

## Exchange, Kula, and Mutual Calibration, 1926–1929

During Huizinga’s spring 1926 American tour, his itinerary carried him through Philadelphia, Baltimore, Washington and Chapel Hill. At Harvard his route crossed Malinowski’s: Malinowski was himself in the United States on a Rockefeller-supported visit, and his report places Huizinga in a Harvard seminar where discussions similar to Malinowski’s theoretical programme were repeated.[^2]

The encounter met an existing repertoire. Huizinga later remembered reaching history from Sanskrit and comparative linguistics by a ‘salto mortale’; he also admitted that his early teaching preferred a vivid story to critical groundedness (Huizinga, 1947). Long before the American encounter, the 1919 first edition of *Herfsttij* had called symbolic thought, from an ethnological standpoint, `een zeer primitieve geestesfunctie` and characterized *het primitieve denken* by a weak perception of identity boundaries, using that ethnological model to account for medieval symbolism.[^24] His Indian papers also used *primitief* for earlier intellectual or medical layers.[^3] In 1921, reviewing Wells’s attempt to extend world history from planetary and biological development into human history, Huizinga objected that geological or biological *ontwikkeling* had little to do with ‘de functie van het historisch begrijpen’; a wider chronology did not by itself yield historical understanding (Huizinga, 1921). His critique of Spengler made the same problem comparative: exact homologies across civilizations forced particulars onto the Procrustean bed of a system, while *Preussentum und Sozialismus* turned the operation political through a *historisch-ethnografische tegenstelling* between English and Prussian character (Huizinga, 1921; *Verzamelde Werken* IV, pp. 451, 461). On 7 April 1926, only days before his transatlantic crossing—and six days before a surviving letter places him aboard the *Berengaria*[^25]—he attacked *evolutie* as a general historical principle, defined cultural history as social morphology concerned with what connects people to one another rather than with what holds them together internally, and placed ethnology among the special cultural sciences already practising such morphology (Huizinga, 1927: 5–7).

The point is not that Huizinga had already solved the problem Malinowski would pose. In *Herfsttij*, the primitive model itself helped explain medieval symbolism, and verbal efficacy was said to appear in its fullness in primitive culture while persisting into the late Middle Ages.[^24] Huizinga arrived with both an older comparative shortcut and a methodological reason not to let sequence or a general term settle historical meaning in advance. Malinowski entered that tension rather than creating it.

On Malinowski’s side, the Rockefeller report placed anthropology inside a larger American argument about social science and policy. Under ‘The functional view of Anthropology’ it described ideas, customs and institutions through the work they performed within an integral cultural scheme, and said that similar theoretical discussions were repeated at Harvard. By 19 May, writing home from Boston, Huizinga was already calling him ‘mijn nieuwen vriend Malinowski’ and promising to tell his son more about him after returning.[^2] Malinowski, he wrote, had points of resemblance with Jolles but also ‘groote verschillen’. Huizinga had heard him inside a new social-scientific setting and was already testing resemblance against difference.

Huizinga’s own American notes place the functional vocabulary in a wider setting. In *Amerika levend en denkend*, internally dated October 1926 and published the following year, he described American social science as pluralist and hostile to a single explanatory ground. Summarizing Dewey, he wrote that all action was *interactie, wederzijdsche handeling* and that morality, economic life and scientific thought were *sociale functies*; at the same time he resisted making history mere *hand- en spandiensten* for sociology.[^22] By December, *functional* belonged to a wider repertoire. The pair relation made it concrete through books and historical problems.

Nearly seven months after the Boston letter, Huizinga recalled another conversation about ‘ethnological problems’, this time along the Haarlemsche Trekvaart. On 12 December 1926 he thanked Malinowski for a letter followed by three books, ‘the small, the medium and the big one’. The parcel included *Crime and Custom in Savage Society*, *Sex and Repression in Savage Society* and *Argonauts of the Western Pacific*.[^4] Huizinga began with *Crime and Custom*, made the calf joke, then turned serious: Malinowski’s ‘outlook on ethnological problems’ was ‘just the one I can profit by for my historical method’. Nineteen days later he made the affinity explicit: ‘We emphasize the functional side of cultural phenomena.’ Feudal law, troubadour culture and the efficacy of words followed immediately.[^4]

The order matters. Huizinga did not receive an abstract doctrine and then apply it. He chose among physical books, returned from a book to their earlier walk, and from the walk to his own historical craft. The force of words was not a new object: in the 1919 first edition of *Herfsttij*, Huizinga had already described belief in `de werking van het gesproken woord` as fully manifest in primitive culture and persisting in late-medieval blessings, magical formulas and legal formulas.[^24] What Malinowski supplied was a denser configuration in which rules, obligations and effects had to be followed through relations. The Trobriand world behind *Argonauts* and the other books would remain in the pair corridor long enough for Kula itself to reappear in Huizinga’s 1933 historical comparison.

Malinowski’s published self-description shows why this was not a clean break on his side either. Earlier in 1926, in the dedication to *Myth in Primitive Psychology*, adapted from his 1925 Frazer Lecture, he recalled illness interrupting physical and chemical research, *The Golden Bough*, and becoming ‘bound to the service of Frazerian anthropology’ (Malinowski, 1926b: 5–6). His early writings place that conversion story inside a broader Central European formation (Thornton and Skalník, 1993). *Crime and Custom* still named ‘primitive law’, but its account of how rules bound moved through reciprocal services, kin relations, public knowledge and obligations of return (Malinowski, 1926a). The title preserved a classificatory object while the body shifted the burden of explanation. His attack on ‘primitive mentality’ similarly used Trobriand agriculture, navigation and technical knowledge against allowing the label to determine the contents of mind in advance (Tilley, 2010).

For their exchange, the important move was from what primitive or savage societies were said to be toward what relations observed there could do in another historical argument. Huizinga’s 31 December phrase ‘so-called primitive culture’ immediately opened onto vanity, generosity, friendship, words and feudal relations. Elsewhere a working slip called knightly games ‘beleving der Feodaltijd’.[^4] Malinowski did not supply those historical objects from scratch. His ethnography attached them to observed circuits of reciprocity, obligation, trust and exchange; Huizinga tested that denser configuration’s historical reach.[^17]

Fifteen months later, on 29 March 1928, *Sex and Repression in Savage Society* gave Huizinga ‘new visions concerning the very origins of culture’, while *Crime and Custom* remained perhaps more useful ‘for historical use’.[^5] The distinction separated two jobs that the older category could easily conflate. ‘Origins of culture’ retained the promise that living difference might disclose beginnings. ‘Historical use’ valued relations described in the Trobriands without requiring the Trobrianders simply to stand as Europe’s past. The same year, the third edition of *Mensch en menigte in Amerika* described modern urban revivalism as `het voortleven van een primitieve geesteshouding in een economisch hoog ontwikkeld milieu` and compared its credulity and excitability with the medieval popular mind.[^23] In the same letter to Malinowski Huizinga resisted one Freudian argument by invoking ‘the immense complexity of the smallest social phenomenon’, then generalized about Freud and Marx through the antisemitic stereotype of a ‘Talmudic mind’. Complexity and flattening sat on the same page. The letter also sent Huizinga’s remarks on American culture to Malinowski for judgment.

Malinowski replied on 15 August, ‘We often speak of you’, and called the reading of Huizinga’s American notes ‘a most stimulating event’: ‘we read them aloud’.[^6] A historian who had been receiving books on savage society sent his own diagnosis of contemporary America back to the anthropologist; Malinowski’s household made it an oral text. The traffic was therefore reciprocal without being symmetrical. Huizinga’s comparative practice was being altered by ethnographic relations and examples. The surviving evidence does not show Huizinga remaking functional anthropology, but it does show Malinowski repeatedly engaging a historian who was carrying anthropology across a disciplinary boundary and returning his own work for judgment.

Nine months later, on 13 May 1929, after receiving *The Sexual Life of Savages in North-Western Melanesia*, Huizinga said he felt ‘quite at home in the Trobriands’. He then described the course he had taught the previous winter to students at Delft Technical University, organized around *homo faber*: it ran from ‘the stage of savage life’ through old Aryan culture to Rome and its decay.[^7] Thick familiarity and stadial order coexisted. Malinowski’s ethnography could make one position in the sequence internally dense without immediately dissolving the sequence itself.

Huizinga’s 1929 methodological writings made the same coexistence explicit in another register. In ‘Over een definitie van het begrip geschiedenis’, ‘the primitive cultures of the whole world’ had become components of modern civilization; yet a history adequate to that civilization still had to answer to critically scientific verification (Huizinga, 1929: 156–168). In ‘De taak der cultuurgeschiedenis’, religious studies and ethnology could define forms such as myth, consecration, sacred action, contest-play and secret association, while cultural history followed their *werking* in historical events. Service, honour, loyalty and obedience might each be objects of sociology, but their systematic treatment remained insufficient unless cultural history showed their changing operation and form across centuries and countries. The exchange had not produced a common method. It had made the division of comparative labour harder to ignore.

## Travel and Comparative Method, 1929–1931

By the time Huizinga travelled to Java, Bali and Hong Kong in the winter of 1930–31, the Trobriands had become familiar to him through Malinowski’s books. The comparison that matters is therefore not a symmetry of destinations. It is a contrast between ways in which encounters with living difference became knowledge. Writing retrospectively in *Mijn weg tot de historie*, Huizinga contrasted the Asian journey, remembered ‘entirely’ from memory after an injury to his right eye, with the day-to-day notes of his 1926 American trip (Huizinga, 1947). The surviving American archive shows what those earlier notes became: cards on frontier history, Indian policy and the Ghost Dance leading into comparative prose on America and Europe.[^8]

Malinowski’s Trobriand books rested on repeated field observation, language and the reconstruction of relations among living actors. Huizinga’s travel could feed comparison, but his historical work moved through a different chain: notes or memory, reading, cards, surviving texts, images and later acts of verification. Both made distant worlds portable. They did not make them answerable in the same way. That difference is more useful than forcing Java or Bali into a common itinerary: the very portability of peoples described through civilizational or developmental language depended on distinct practices of inscription and checking.

Huizinga’s work on Philip the Good makes the historian’s side of that contrast concrete. In November 1929 he asked the museum in Gotha for a good photograph of the duke’s portrait. The surrounding dossier compared copies, reconstructed relations among versions, followed what museums and photographic suppliers could make available, and treated preservation itself as part of the historical problem. A newly recovered autograph letter could correct an existing historical image; a photographic reproduction was useful only inside a transmission chain that could itself be checked.[^10] Working notes from the same period distinguish collecting or establishing historical material from ordering it under a point of view.[^11] Malinowski could return to relations among living interlocutors; Huizinga’s comparisons remained answerable to damaged survival, copying and correction.

Two strands of Huizinga scholarship sharpen the contrast. Work on the making of *Herfsttij* has made his selective source use and material organization objects of historiography (Small, 2019; van der Lem, 2019). Arnade (2019) emphasizes anthropological affinities in Huizinga’s treatment of ritual and cultural form; Vale (2021) warns that an *anthropologist avant la lettre* can obscure the philological and linguistic formation that continued to shape his historical work. The pair relation puts those claims in the same frame. Anthropology changed the questions and comparative materials available to Huizinga more readily than it replaced the historical practices by which his claims became corrigible.

## Institutions: Leiden, Anthropology, and the Human Sciences, 1931

By 1931 *primitive* was not disappearing from the human sciences. It remained operational in disputes over law and mentality, philanthropic programmes, museum classification and institutional design (Biehn, 2009; Chimisso, 2000; Pels, 2022). The Leiden Centre proposal turned that wider reorganization into a local problem of buildings, collections, languages, personnel, money and disciplinary principle.

The memorandum did not begin its life when Huizinga sent it abroad. On 17 July Van Vollenhoven told him that the paper had already been shown locally, including to Van Eerde, and revised; Snouck Hurgronje was proposed as another reader.[^12] The object that reached Malinowski was therefore already a Leiden composite, altered by local circulation before it entered the Huizinga–Malinowski correspondence. Its classificatory language belonged to a practical attempt to gather existing museums, institutes, languages and colonial expertises into an institution.

Huizinga had already put part of that institutional map into print. In June 1929, discussing university ‘role division’, he reproduced the claim that Leiden’s chairs in Indonesian languages and in *land- en volkenkunde van Nederlandsch Indië* were ‘een privilege der Leidsche Universiteit’, explicitly linked to the university’s collections and chairs in colonial law.[^20]

On 19 July 1931 Huizinga sent Malinowski ‘a paper which I made up’ for a Centre of Anthropological Research. The letter followed a visit by Van Sickle and Tracy Kittredge, who had come ‘to look into the matter’.[^12] Leiden already possessed an ethnographical museum, Oriental and language institutes, legal and *adat* expertise tied to colonial administration, and other dispersed scholarly resources; Huizinga asked Malinowski for judgment and, if favourable, Rockefeller help. The supporting packet comprised Huizinga’s seven-page signed memorandum and a two-page signed annex by A. W. Nieuwenhuis.[^12]

The papers placed Leiden’s resources on a civilizational map. Java could be ‘high culture’, Bali an unusually interesting Hindu remnant, other populations a ‘backward stage’. Nieuwenhuis’s annex went further: ‘Ethnographical objects as data for psychological research about primitive or semi-cultured races’.[^13] Wicker-work, textiles, metalwork and carving could become evidence of mental capacities; those capacities could bear on development, administration and indigenous industry. The packet therefore joined classification to institutional design and possible intervention. Huizinga’s 1926 suspicion of general terms had not produced a linear retreat from developmental language; he was now helping to route a programme in which such labels still organized objects and populations.

Huizinga’s own 1930 lectures set a limit from another direction. In a lecture sequence on seventeenth-century Dutch civilization he admitted social, economic, political and, ‘if one wishes’, ethnographic conditions, then denied that their enumeration completely explained a civilization.[^11] The historical reservation now entered an institutional setting in which anthropology itself had to say what would make a list of resources into a discipline.

Eleven days after Huizinga sent the memorandum, Malinowski replied on 30 July. Van Sickle had already spoken to him about Leiden during a recent weekend stay; with only that briefing Malinowski had expressed unreserved interest and enthusiasm. After the memorandum itself arrived, however, support became conditional on reading. He still intended to help, but postponed his Rockefeller intervention until he had made ‘a few comments on your Memorandum’ and heard Huizinga’s answer.[^14]

The comments turned immediately to constitution. Who would ‘carry on the work and inspire the institution with their spirit’? What ‘central aim or principle’ would hold it together? Malinowski worried that museum buildings and equipment dominated the scheme, rejected anthropology as a *mixtum compositum* of archaeology, collectioneering and other activities, asked for ‘a definite social science’, urged Leiden to ‘sociologise’, and joined living language to living culture. His judgment of Huizinga himself was equally explicit: ‘Your method in history strikes me as being the right sort of really anthropological approach, so that if you could play even a teaching part it would be invaluable.’[^14]

The audit exposed a distinction the letters could postpone. Collections and categories could identify an object of research; they could not supply the personnel, organizing principle or disciplinary competence Malinowski wanted. Thirteen days later, on 12 August, Huizinga conceded the ‘weakest spots in our scheme’. The memorandum contained ‘a bit of window-dressing, or rather of a draft on the future’. Reorganizing the Ethnographical Museum and bringing it closer to the Eastern institutes might allow a centre to emerge; making the movement toward sociology too explicit would scare ‘our own men’, though Huizinga personally welcomed that direction. Leiden’s leading *adat* scholar was ‘no anthropologist proper’. ‘I myself am only a benevolent outsider.’[^1]

Outsiderhood was therefore produced inside proximity. Huizinga could assemble museums, languages, colonial legal expertise and comparative questions; Malinowski could call his historical method ‘really anthropological’. But the proposed centre still lacked the disciplinary principle and personnel Malinowski demanded. The same human-scientific traffic that carried *primitive* across books, objects and institutions made authority over its use more explicit rather than less.

The audit did not stop that traffic. On 15 September an unpublished Yale letter from Huizinga to Malinowski survives in the public inventory with the incipit ‘I am sending you a volume just pubished’.[^15] Nineteen days later Malinowski’s reply thanked Huizinga for *Encyclopaedie der Karaïben*; a pupil studying the couvade was already using it. Malinowski enclosed a Bertrand Russell review of Huizinga’s book and said he was taking Huizinga’s 12 August institute letter to Paris, where he would raise the Leiden matter with Van Sickle if necessary.[^15] A book moved from Huizinga through Malinowski to a pupil, a review moved back, and an institutional letter moved onward toward Rockefeller. The boundary had been clarified without closing the corridor.

## 1933 as a Hinge: Solutions, Limits, and Divergence

The exchange did not culminate in one shared method. Malinowski’s work had increasingly made classificatory labels carry less explanatory weight than relations, function, reciprocity, obligation and situated knowledge, while his Leiden audit tied those practices to disciplinary personnel and organization. Huizinga increasingly let specific relations travel into historical comparison while retaining source and transmission checks and declining to convert that traffic into a claim to anthropological authority. Neither response required *primitive* to disappear, and neither prevented older developmental uses from returning.

The personal and scholarly corridor remained dense into 1933. In November–December 1932 the surviving record contains six direct letters in little more than three weeks; Malinowski thanked Huizinga for his article on the poetic and theological in Alanus de Insulis, while Huizinga wrote back through a mixture of scholarly and personal matters.[^18] On 30 January 1933, in an unpublished Yale letter, Malinowski said he was willing to write for *De Gids* and considered beginning in Dutch and having Huizinga correct his style.[^18] Their proximity had become linguistic as well as intellectual.

On 8 February 1933, as Rector Magnificus on Leiden University’s 358th dies natalis, Huizinga delivered *Over de grenzen van spel en ernst in de cultuur*.[^21] He moved from the Trobriand Kula exchange to Mauss’s potlatch and then to Philip the Good’s 1454 Burgundian *Vœu du Faisan*. The bridge was deliberately narrower than a stack of parallels. Huizinga introduced the Burgundian case not for its ‘play-character in general’ but as a specific example of *wedijverend, wederkeerig weeldevertoon en spilziek onthaal*: hosts took turns, passed a wreath, escalated splendour, and were finally outdone by Philip. Only after specifying that sequence did he call the case ‘not so very far’ from a potlatch. Kula remained Kula and Burgundy remained Burgundy; what crossed the comparison was a relation of reciprocal competitive display.

The same year made the institutional limit equally concrete. Huizinga had chaired the Academy’s Section of Letters since 11 November 1929. During Malinowski’s candidature for membership, Huizinga wrote on 23 May: ‘Being no anthropologist I refrained from taking a leading part in your candidature’, though he signed it.[^16] The verb ‘refrained’ records an action rather than mere modesty. Huizinga could support Malinowski from a senior Academy office while refusing to turn intellectual proximity into disciplinary leadership.

Late 1933 shows why neither response counts as a completed solution. On 27 October Huizinga wrote Malinowski that he had come across a review of Sjoerd Hofstra’s *Differenzierungserscheinungen in einigen afrikanischen Gruppen: Ein Beitrag zur Frage der primitiven Individualität*; Malinowski replied three days later.[^19] In the Amsterdam lecture Huizinga delivered on 6 November, he began from a problem of naming. The great objects of cultural history, he wrote, were so complex and diffuse that they could not properly bear a name or be grasped as a closed unity, yet without names history could not make them visible to the mind (Huizinga, 1934; *Verzamelde Werken* IV, p. 341). He then used Hofstra’s citation of a prophet among *natuurvolken* who called people back to old loyalties because the ideal age lay in the past. The ethnographic example supplied a relation—return to an idealized past—to a general historical claim. It did not require the people described as primitive to stand simply as Europe’s past.

The change between 1929 and 1933 is therefore visible in what carried the comparison. In 1929 ‘the stage of savage life’ could itself organize a historical sequence.[^7] In 1933 Huizinga moved from Kula through potlatch to Burgundy by specifying circulation, rivalry, reciprocal display and prestige; in the Hofstra episode he imported an orientation toward the past rather than a civilizational stage. *Primitive* remained in active circulation, but more of the proof now stood in named relations.

Locher later argued that Malinowski had not modified Huizinga’s global image of primitive culture (Locher, 1958). The archive makes that judgment compatible with change at another level. A large classificatory image could persist while the conclusions once compressed inside it were increasingly carried by specific relations, source checks and disciplinary distinctions. The question is not whether Malinowski replaced Huizinga’s primitive. It is what their sustained exchange forced each comparison to specify before the category could support a conclusion.

The surviving exchange makes 1933 a hinge rather than a terminal solution. Huizinga’s play-centred line expands from 1934 into another corpus; the later trajectories of the two scholars are no longer comparably visible in the same pair archive. Following them symmetrically would require a different evidentiary base. What can be established at the hinge is narrower: anthropology could reorganize historical questions without transferring disciplinary authority, and a classificatory vocabulary could persist after losing much of its former power to settle the comparison by itself.

Leiden had already given that change an institutional form. Shared questions could move between anthropology and history; authority over their comparison did not move so easily. ‘Benevolent outsider’ was Huizinga’s name for that position. The word *primitive* remained. More and more of the proof stood outside it.
