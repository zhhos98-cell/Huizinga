# `Primitive`, 1900–1940: interwar historiography delta

Date: 2026-09-04  
Purpose: fill the key interval between late-Victorian primitive and postwar deauthorization for the Huizinga–Malinowski article.

## Core finding

The strongest current historiography treats 1900–1940 neither as the survival of an unchanged Victorian `primitive` nor as a clean transition in which fieldwork abolished evolutionism. It is a period in which the category is **epistemically reworked, institutionally redistributed, and increasingly reflexive while remaining lexically powerful**.

A useful shorthand is:

`PRIMITIVE RETAINED`
+
`OBJECT OF INQUIRY CHANGES`
+
`LOCAL EVIDENCE THICKENS`
+
`DISCIPLINARY JURISDICTIONS HARDEN`
+
`MACRO TEMPORAL HIERARCHY REMAINS UNEVENLY AVAILABLE`.

## 1. Durkheim / Mauss, 1903: classification becomes object as well as method

`De quelques formes primitives de classification` (1903) is usually remembered for the thesis that society supplies categories of thought. Peter Pels's 2022 historiographical rereading is more useful here. He argues that the work marks an epistemic shift from collecting objects, facts, and bodies in a natural-historical mode toward studying the categories by which societies collectively represent themselves. Yet `primitive classification` still asserts a colonial dichotomy. The same move can therefore **displace older racial classification while reproducing a primitive/modern division in a new form**.

Pels's methodological lesson is to distinguish what a classification:
- describes;
- constructs;
- intervenes in.

These dimensions can change at different historical moments. This is an excellent model for the Huizinga article's paragraph-level counter-proof.

Reference: Peter Pels, `Classification revisited: On time, methodology and position in decolonizing anthropology`, *Anthropological Theory* 22.1 (2022), 78–101.

## 2. Lévy-Bruhl: `primitive mentality` as disciplinary battlefield

Cristina Chimisso's history of the interwar Sorbonne shows that the controversy over Lévy-Bruhl's *La mentalité primitive* was not only a doctrine about non-Western minds. It was also a struggle among philosophy, sociology, psychology, ethnology, and history of science over who could scientifically study mind. The 1923 Société Française de Philosophie discussion linked claims about fixity/universality of mind to judgments about the scientific status of ethnology.

This gives `primitive` a second function directly relevant to `outsider`: it can be a **jurisdictional object**. Disputes about primitive mentality are simultaneously disputes about disciplinary competence.

Reference: Cristina Chimisso, `The mind and the faculties: the controversy over 'primitive mentality' and the struggle for disciplinary space at the inter-war Sorbonne`, *History of the Human Sciences* 13.3 (2000).

## 3. Malinowski: the adjective survives while the explanatory centre moves

Malinowski's own 1925 `Primitive Law` still uses `primitive economics`, `savages`, and practical colonial language. It therefore cannot support a retrospective story in which fieldwork simply expels the category. Yet the piece insists on empirical facts over uncontrolled conjecture.

His attack on Lévy-Bruhl is even more revealing. As Helen Tilley stresses in `Global Histories, Vernacular Science, and African Genealogies` (*Isis* 101.1, 2010), Malinowski argued in the 1920s/1930 that no people, however `primitive`, lacked scientific attitude or reason. Trobriand agriculture, healing, astronomy, navigation, terminology, and sailing knowledge became evidence against a blanket prelogical mentality. Tilley's history places this within the wider emergence of scholarly interest in `native knowledge` / `vernacular science` at the turn of the century.

Thus:

`primitive` can remain as population/cultural vocabulary
while
`primitive mind` loses the power to settle the content of knowledge in advance.

This is exactly the type of inferential deauthorization needed for the Huizinga argument.

Reference: Helen Tilley, `Global Histories, Vernacular Science, and African Genealogies; or, Is the History of Science Ready for the World?`, *Isis* 101.1 (2010).

## 4. 1937 Malinowski / Julius Lips: primitive becomes reversible

Malinowski's 1937 introduction to Julius Lips, *The Savage Hits Back*, is a strong late-interwar control. He describes anthropology as learning to `find the human being in the savage` and to `discover the primitive in the highly sophisticated Westerner of today`; he also says the dividing line between savage and civilized is not easy to draw.

This does not abolish the vocabulary. It makes its direction reversible. The primitive is no longer securely confined to an external population occupying an earlier stage; it can be found within the modern West.

For the Huizinga project, this is very close to what happens when Kula/potlatch/Burgundian ceremony are compared through relation, prestige, rivalry, gift, and play rather than by simply assigning them to one primitive stage.

Primary control: Bronislaw Malinowski, introduction to Julius Lips, *The Savage Hits Back* (1937), reprinted in *HAU* 5.3 (2015).

## 5. Interwar philanthropy / institution

Kersten Jacobson Biehn's history of American anthropology in the 1920s and 1930s shows that nature/nurture disputes about `monkeys, babies, idiots, and primitives` were also shaped by philanthropic foundation support. This reinforces the point that primitive was not only a theory-word but an institutional funding and programme object.

Reference: Kersten Jacobson Biehn, `“Monkeys, babies, idiots” and “primitives”: Nature–nurture debates and philanthropic foundation support for American anthropology in the 1920s and 1930s`, *Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences* 45.3 (2009), 219–235.

This is directly adjacent to the Huizinga–Malinowski Rockefeller scene and should be used as historiographical support, not as a substitute for pair-level evidence.

## 6. Current interpretation of interwar `primitive`

The historiographical sequence is therefore better represented as:

### older reading
`evolutionary primitive` -> `fieldwork` -> `modern anthropology`

### stronger current reading
`primitive as classificatory infrastructure`
-> `native categories become research objects`
-> `fieldwork / vernacular evidence thickens local knowledge`
-> `primitive/modern macro distinctions remain unevenly active`
-> `disciplinary and institutional struggles determine who may authorize the comparison`.

The consequence for Huizinga/Malinowski is significant. Their exchange should be located **inside** this reworking, not described as a historian borrowing an already-modern anthropology.

Malinowski himself is part of the transformation. He retains older vocabulary, attacks some of its inferential uses, builds new empirical warrants, and later makes `primitive` partially reversible. Huizinga meets anthropology during precisely this unstable interval.

## 7. Primitive ↔ outsider

This interwar historiography strengthens the current article thesis:

> `primitive` becomes less capable of serving as a universally self-authorizing bridge at exactly the moment disciplinary jurisdictions become more explicit.

Therefore `benevolent outsider` can be read as more than modesty. It is a self-location produced by a knowledge order in which:

`shared vocabulary / object`
!=
`shared research warrant`
!=
`shared institutional authority`.

The 1931 Leiden Centre is where these three layers are forced to line up and fail to line up completely.

## Counter-proof rule

Do not write:
- Malinowski abolished primitive;
- fieldwork automatically produced coevalness;
- institutionalization simply replaced colonial anthropology with professional science;
- `primitive mentality` was only a racist doctrine with no internal epistemic dispute.

Write instead:
- the category's semantic availability persisted;
- specific inferences became harder to license;
- local empirical richness and macro hierarchy could coexist;
- primitive objects were also disciplinary-jurisdictional objects;
- increased intersubjectivity did not erase asymmetry.
