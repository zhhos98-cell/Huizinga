A fresh recheck of the raw OCR exposes a potentially article-level formulation at the opening of the English prose. The manuscript moves from the inequality between American knowledge of European history and European knowledge of American history into a methodological statement about historical understanding. The present OCR, **not yet diplomatic**, reads approximately:

- `If history is to proceed, as it always should, from facts to vision, it stands in need of certain large and general conceptions which must serve as the frame into which to fit ... endless variety of history.`
- `These general ideas may be vague[s], they often lack exactitude, but they are indispensable.`
- immediately thereafter: `One of these primary notions is that of a conflict ...`
- and then: `The more primitive, the more readily it will evoke our sense of the heroic, ... or romantic and the more easily it will graft itself upon memory.`

The exact final words around `heroic / tragic? / romantic` and several surrounding readings remain OCR-unstable. **No direct quotation from this list may enter Draft 04 until checked against the scan.**

## Published 1918 control: same argument-family, not yet the same manuscript state

The opening of Huizinga's 1918 `Een historische tegenstelling getoetst aan de Amerikaansche geschiedenis. I. Individualisme` gives a strong structural control. Its first section is headed `De behoefte aan algemeene maatstaven, om de geschiedenis te begrijpen`. Huizinga says that historical understanding reduces events to a `dramatisch schema`, a simple form; historical thought uses only a small number of such `schema's en vormen`, derived from ancient and European history, and he proposes to test two of them against American history. The first is the old/new conflict; later sections turn to individualism/association.

The published sequence therefore runs:

`need for general measures/forms → a few historical schemata → old/new conflict → test against American history`.

The `chunk_018` English manuscript runs, provisionally:

`American/European historical knowledge → facts to vision → large/general conceptions, vague/inexact but indispensable → conflict as a primary notion → primitive as intensifier inside the conflict schema`.

This is too close in order and function to treat as generic thematic resemblance. It is a **strong same-argument-family relation**.

Bibliographic control sharpens the 1918 state: Huizinga's bibliography records the 1918 De Gids text as an expansion (`Uitwerking`) of his KNAW address `Individualisme en Associatie in de Amerikaansche geschiedenis`, delivered **10 June 1918**, with minor changes when reprinted in *Mensch en menigte in Amerika*.

## Important textual difference

The published 1918 Dutch text contains many active `primitief` classifications, but the opening methodological sequence as currently controlled does **not** simply reproduce the English manuscript's immediate juxtaposition of methodological schemata and `primitive`.

In the published text:

- `primitieve traditie` appears in the discussion of old European powers;
- organization is said to represent both a `primitieve` and a highly developed condition;
- the next section is explicitly headed `Individualisme ... draagt er een primitief karakter`;
- Huizinga says the American case lacks a phase of `primitieve collectivisme`.

Thus `primitive` is structurally central to the published American comparison, but the English working state appears—pending scan verification—to make a still more explicit methodological move: a broad historical conception may be admitted as vague/inexact yet indispensable, and `primitive` is then deployed inside one such schematic operation.

If diplomatic reading confirms this, it is unusually direct evidence for the article's central problem: **how much inferential work a broad historical classification could perform even when its lack of exactitude was itself acknowledged.**

## Dating and occasion: keep open

Do **not** identify the English manuscript as the 1918 KNAW speech, a 1917–18 course manuscript, a 1926 American lecture, or a post-1926 rewrite without further evidence.

Known constraints:

1. Huizinga taught an America course in 1917–18; the 1918 article developed from the 10 June 1918 KNAW address.
2. Huizinga Online's calendar again assigns a **17-session America course in 1925–26** to `[H.A. 28]`, ending 16 March 1926, shortly before Huizinga sailed for the United States in April.
3. The April–June 1926 calendar securely records the American itinerary, meetings and visits, but the presently inspected entries do **not** supply a matching public-lecture title for this English manuscript.
4. The English manuscript addresses Americans directly (`average educated Americans ... European history`) and contrasts European/American historical knowledge. This makes an Anglophone audience plausible but does **not** prove delivery in the United States.
5. `chunk_018` is heterogeneous. The source-card apparatus includes later American scholarship/material as well as the synthetic manuscript, so the chunk's physical aggregation cannot by itself date every component.
6. Huizinga's America materials were repeatedly reused. Huizinga Online assigns 1917–18, 1925–26 and later America teaching/material to the H.A./HUI 28 family. H.A. 28 is therefore a family locator, not a dating device.

Current best formulation: **a handwritten English reworking in the same argument-family as the 1918 `Individualisme` essay, preserved among Huizinga's America working papers; exact state/date/occasion unresolved.**
