# 1933 `Renaissance en Nieuwe Tijd` naming/category control — 2026-09-05

Purpose: recover a load-bearing methodological passage from the same 6 November 1933 lecture already used for the Hofstra example. This passage bears directly on Draft 04's claim that a classificatory name can remain necessary while losing the capacity to function as a closed explanatory unit.

## Source

Johan Huizinga, `Renaissance en Nieuwe Tijd`, Amsterdam lecture delivered 6 November 1933, published in *Neophilologus* 19 (1934), reprinted in *Verzamelde Werken* IV, DBNL TEI `sources/dbnl/verzamelde_werken/huiz003verz05_01.xml`, VW p. 341.

Recovered in the now-frozen priority review at `archive/research_runs/dbnl/article_dbnl_vw_priority_units_review_2026-09-04.md`.

## Controlled passage

Huizinga opens the relevant methodological discussion:

`Cultuurgeschiedenis is een lastig ding. Haar objecten, de groote verschijnselen van opgang, verandering, aard en val van een beschaving, zijn zoo complex en zoo diffuus, dat zij eigenlijk geen naam kunnen dragen, niet als gesloten eenheid kunnen worden begrepen. Doch zoolang zij geen naam hebben, kan de historie ze niet zichtbaar maken voor den geest.`

He immediately adds that the mind does not wait for history's verdict. Names such as Renaissance, Reformation, Revolution, Restoration and Risorgimento often spring from the wish and imagination of the age itself, `dien grooten mythograaf`.

The lecture then develops the `re-` structure and states that when humanity means the future it often cries `terug`; the footnote supplies Hofstra/Willoughby's prophet among `natuurvolken` as an example of return to old loyalties and an ideal age in the past.

## Article function

**BODY / METHOD + CATEGORY CONTROL.** This is unusually close to Draft 04's conceptual problem without being a retrospective paraphrase imposed on Huizinga:

- complex cultural objects resist being grasped as closed named unities;
- history nevertheless needs names to render them mentally visible;
- names can arise from actors' wishes/imagination rather than from a final historical judgment;
- a name is therefore epistemically necessary without being equivalent to a self-sufficient explanation.

This should not be turned into a claim that Huizinga was theorizing the word `primitive` in this passage. The article's use is second-order and explicit: the same 1933 historian who still circulated `primitive` was articulating a general problem of historical naming in which a name was necessary but could not close the object.

## Article decision

Add 1–2 sentences at the start of the existing late-1933 `Renaissance en Nieuwe Tijd` paragraph, immediately before the `when humanity means the future it often cries back` sentence.

Safe wording:

`The same lecture began from a problem of naming. Huizinga wrote that the great objects of cultural history were so complex and diffuse that they could not properly bear a name or be grasped as a closed unity, yet without names history could not make them visible to the mind; such names often arose from the wishes and imagination of the age itself (Huizinga, 1934).`

Then retain the existing Hofstra relation sentence.

This strengthens the article's conclusion without claiming the passage defines `primitive`: category/name remains usable and necessary, while closed explanatory sufficiency is denied.
