# `Primitive`: full-history and contemporary historiography sweep

Date: 2026-09-04  
Status: **research synthesis for Huizinga–Malinowski article; historiography-first, not generic anthropology history**

## Governing distinction

The history of `primitive` must not be written as a single lexical biography or as a simple rise-and-fall story. At least three historical objects need to be kept apart:

1. `primitive` as an actor category meaning first/original/early, often positively normative;
2. the modern human-scientific `primitive`, in which living cultural/geographical difference could be made to stand for developmental or historical anteriority;
3. `primitivism` / `primitive` as a second-order historiographical object through which historians reconstruct classifications, temporal politics, evidence regimes, institutions, and the history of the human sciences.

The central current-historiographical question is therefore not merely `who believed in primitive?` but:

> **What inferential work was the category licensed to perform, on what evidence, through which institutions, and how did that license change even when the vocabulary survived?**

This formulation is especially important for the Huizinga–Malinowski article.

---

## I. Long prehistory: `primitive` before the anthropological primitive

The English word derives from Latin `primitivus`, first/earliest of its kind. Medieval and early-modern usage could mean original, primary, first-age, or ancestral. In Christian historical writing the `primitive Church` was not a backward social formation but an originary and frequently normative model: purity, orthodoxy, discipline, and apostolic practice could be measured against it. Current historians of eighteenth-century religion still reconstruct `primitive Christianity` in exactly this sense; Robert G. Ingram (2018) shows that the primitive church remained a standard of orthodoxy and orthopraxy, while Geordan Hammond (2014) traces John Wesley's patristic programme of primitive Christianity.

**Control:** `primitive = first/original` is much older than `primitive = living backward people`. The latter must be historically produced rather than projected backward.

Useful historiography:
- Robert G. Ingram, *Reformation without End* (2018), ch. 12, `The religion of the first ages: primitivism and the primitive Church`.
- Geordan Hammond, *John Wesley in America* (2014), ch. 1.
- Anthony Pagden, *The Fall of Natural Man* (1982/1986), for the pre-Enlightenment vocabularies of `barbarian` / `civil` and comparative ethnology.

---

## II. Sixteenth to eighteenth centuries: recognition, barbarism, and stadialization

Anthony Pagden's classic history of comparative ethnology reconstructs the long New World problem before the mature anthropological `primitive`: Europeans had to classify Amerindian peoples through inherited categories of barbarism, civility, natural law, slavery, language, and reason. This matters because it prevents a retrospective assumption that the nineteenth-century category was already fully present in sixteenth-century encounter.

By the eighteenth century, however, conjectural/stadial history created a much more systematic conversion between subsistence, social form, manners, and historical position. Ronald Meek's *Social Science and the Ignoble Savage* (1976) remains the classic reconstruction of four-stages theory; more recent work, especially Silvia Sebastiani's *The Scottish Enlightenment: Race, Gender, and the Limits of Progress* (2013) and Deborah Valenze's *The Invention of Scarcity* (2023), shows how the stages were simultaneously accounts of universal history and classifications of regional/racial/gendered difference.

**Key transition:** chronological primitivity and cultural difference become increasingly convertible. A people encountered in the present can be used as evidence for an earlier human stage.

**Control:** eighteenth-century stadial history is a major genealogy of the modern primitive, but it should not simply be equated with the later professional anthropological category.

## III. Last third of nineteenth century: consolidation of the modern anthropological primitive

A useful recent dating proposal comes from Erhard Schüttpelz's 2024 reassessment of primitivism: the anthropological category of the `primitive` is not strongly developed before the 1860s and consolidates in the last third of the nineteenth century, alongside imperial escalation and the breakthrough of evolutionist temporal structures. His return to Lovejoy and Boas is important: their distinction between chronological primitivism (earlier in time) and cultural primitivism (simpler peoples/cultures in the present) helps identify the specifically modern operation as their convergence.

This is where the Max Müller repository becomes decisive. The controlled print sequence shows not a static doctrine but a history of inferential license:

- 1870: contemporary African and Melanesian religions categorically `represent` an earlier and more primitive phase `in point of growth`;
- 1878: Müller explicitly tightens source/witness/analogy controls and attacks the easy use of the living `savage of to-day` as chronologically earlier humanity;
- 1882: the earlier categorical sentence is revised to `may or may not represent`;
- 1891/92: he explicitly rejects the equation of contemporary savage peoples with primitive mankind.

At the same time, his classificatory architecture survives and his 1870 `Classify and conquer` is selectively revised in 1882 to `Classify and understand`.

The best interpretation already established in `zhhos98-cell/maxmuller` is:

`OPERATION RETAINED + WARRANT/RHETORIC MODERATED = SELECTIVE RECALIBRATION`.

This sharply improves on a simple `evolutionism -> rejection` narrative. Concept, operation, rhetoric, and evidential warrant can change on different schedules.

## IV. c. 1870–1930s: the primitive thickens rather than simply disappears

Older textbook narratives often oppose Victorian evolutionary `armchair anthropology` to Malinowskian fieldwork. Current history of anthropology has substantially complicated that break. George Stocking's *Victorian Anthropology* (1987) and *After Tylor* (1995) remain foundational for reconstructing the transition from social evolutionism to professional social anthropology. Frederico Delgado Rosa and Han Vermeulen's *Ethnographers Before Malinowski* (2022) now shows that the supposedly armchair era also contained intensive fieldwork, vernacular research, women ethnographers, and Indigenous experts. This matters for any history of `primitive`: sophisticated local knowledge and hierarchical macro categories could coexist.

Peter Pels's `Classification Revisited` (2022) supplies a particularly useful historiographical model. Classification changes historical function: racial methodology, late-colonial object of study, reflexive critique, and twenty-first-century decolonial problem cannot be treated as the same classificatory act. The discipline's move toward native/collective classifications could reproduce colonial asymmetries and simultaneously alter what counted as legitimate anthropological evidence.

For the Huizinga–Malinowski interval, the central formulation should therefore be:

> **Dense ethnography did not instantly abolish `primitive`; it redistributed its explanatory labour.**

Malinowski's `primitive law`, `savage society`, etc. retain the vocabulary while hundreds of pages relocate explanation into reciprocity, kinship, pressure, exchange, language, situated practice, and institutional relations. The adjective remains available while becoming less self-sufficient.

This is exactly the historiographical space in which Huizinga belongs.

## V. Lovejoy as hinge: from `primitive philosophy` to a history of `primitivism`

The Lovejoy repository reveals an unusually important internal transition.

### 1906–1914: first-order macro object

Lovejoy's 1906 `Primitive Energetics` extracts an impersonal-power mechanism from heterogeneous ethnographic cases and promotes it toward `the fundamental concept of primitive philosophy`. Yet his local evidentiary procedure can be highly discriminating: ritual killing is not automatically sacrifice; sacrifice is not automatically communion; type/category is not identical with chronological priority. By 1914 he explicitly distinguishes a strongly warranted non-animistic type proposition from the weaker claim that it was pre-animistic.

The repository's strongest historiographical control is:

> **LOCAL PROOF DISCIPLINE DOES NOT AUTOMATICALLY LICENSE MACRO OBJECT PROMOTION.**

Recent historiography supports this double reading: refined discrimination can coexist with asymmetrical construction of a large object called `primitive`.

### 1920s–1950s: second-order historical object

The same Lovejoy later helps institutionalize `primitivism` as a history-of-ideas research problem. The Hopkins History of Ideas Club repeatedly studies primitivism from 1924 onward; Paul Dumont's 1934 Club talk on `Primitivism in Ancient India` becomes a supplementary essay in Lovejoy and George Boas's *Primitivism and Related Ideas in Antiquity* (1935); Lovejoy explicitly lists `primitivism` among exemplary JHI problems in his 1940 founding programme; George Boas again uses primitivism in 1953 as a problem of intellectual-historical classification.

Thus Lovejoy himself spans:

`primitive as an actor's macro human-scientific object`
→
`primitivism as a second-order historiographical object and classification problem`.

This is a major hinge for a full history of the category.

Important qualification: Lovejoy/Boas's 1935 typology — chronological/cultural, hard/soft primitivism — is analytically powerful but itself historical. It treats primitivism as a recurrent ideational complex and can flatten the institutional, racial, colonial, evidentiary, and jurisdictional differences that later historians make central.

## VI. 1930s–1960s: second-order history and disciplinary rejection coexist

Lovejoy and Boas in 1935 construct a documentary history of `primitivism`, while anthropology still uses `primitive` widely as a first-order term. This overlap is important: the category becomes historicizable before it disappears from ordinary disciplinary usage.

Francis L. K. Hsu's `Rethinking the Concept "Primitive"` (1964) is a key disciplinary self-critique. Hsu calls the concept empirically, theoretically, and practically obsolete except in restricted prehistoric usage, while documenting its continued currency in anthropological books. Ashley Montagu's edited *The Concept of the Primitive* (1968) consolidates this phase of explicit conceptual rejection.

The useful periodization is therefore not `1945 -> primitive disappears`, but a long uneven deauthorization. Schüttpelz's 2024 dating is useful here too: critique begins within anthropology much earlier, while final abandonment belongs to the long postwar decolonization process.

**Control:** lexical abandonment does not guarantee removal of the temporal/classificatory operation. `tribal`, `traditional`, `backward`, `underdeveloped`, and related replacements can inherit parts of the same architecture.

## VII. 1970s–2000s: critical genealogy becomes dominant

This is the historiographical regime most familiar today, but it contains several distinct arguments that should not be collapsed.

### 1. Stadial/social-scientific genealogy
Ronald Meek (1976), Anthony Pagden (1982), and George Stocking (1987/1995) reconstruct the historical formation of conjectural history, comparative ethnology, social evolution, and anthropology rather than treating `primitive` as a timeless Western prejudice.

### 2. Temporal critique
Johannes Fabian's *Time and the Other* (1983) argues that anthropology makes its object through a denial of coevalness: contemporary others are placed in another time. This becomes the most influential formulation of `primitive` as allochronic temporal technology.

### 3. Invented macro object
Adam Kuper's *The Invention of Primitive Society* (1988) argues that there was no original `primitive society` waiting to be discovered; successive anthropological constructions were mirror images of modern concerns.

### 4. Imperial / material / institutional production
Andrew Zimmerman's *Anthropology and Antihumanism in Imperial Germany* (2001) relocates the history from doctrines to museums, objects, spectacles, political economy, empire, and competing human-scientific institutions. Tomoko Masuzawa's *The Invention of World Religions* (2005) reconstructs classifications of religion, language, and race as forms of knowledge rather than transparent descriptors. David Chidester's *Empire of Religion* (2014) follows theory, colonial experts, indigenous informants, circulation, and imperial classification together.

### 5. History-writing and politics of time
Prathama Banerjee's *Politics of Time: 'Primitives' and History-writing in a Colonial Society* (2006) is indispensable for this project because it is explicitly a work of historiography. It asks how colonial Bengal made Santals into a `tribe` and a `primitive within`, and gives travel, circulation, money, practice, and national history-writing their own temporal politics. This is directly relevant to the proposed Huizinga section on travel and knowledge production.

This regime made it impossible to treat `primitive` as merely an erroneous empirical label. It became an instrument for making temporal distance, macro objects, colonial classifications, and institutional jurisdictions.

## VIII. 2010s–2020s: contemporary historiography after the total critique

The strongest current work does **not** simply reverse Fabian/Kuper/postcolonial critique. Instead it tries to retain the politics while recovering historical differentiation.

### A. From ideology to historically variable operation
Peter Pels (2022) asks what different classifications do in different phases of anthropology. This supports separating:

`vocabulary`
!=
`classificatory operation`
!=
`evidential warrant`
!=
`institutional intervention`.

### B. From totalizing critique to archival counter-proof
Frederico Delgado Rosa (2019) explicitly challenges the historiographical distortions created when historians search only for the `faults` predicted by Fabian's allochronism thesis. Vernacular records and language-centred ethnography can produce intersubjective/co-authored knowledge even inside hierarchical anthropology. Rosa and Vermeulen (2022) extend this by recovering pre-Malinowskian fieldworkers, women, and Indigenous experts.

This is a revisionist counter-current, not a reason to declare colonial asymmetry unreal.

### C. From doctrine to standards of evidence
Kristine Palmieri's recent Max Müller work is especially useful to HPS: actors can agree on broad objects such as human origins while fighting over research methods, standards of evidence, and legitimate explanation. This is the historiographical vocabulary that best fits the repo's version history: the conceptual field can persist while the warrant changes.

### D. From `primitive` alone to the historian's own temporal techne
The 2023 *History and Theory* special issue on chronopolitics moves Fabian's problem into historiography itself. Fernando Esposito and Tobias Becker explicitly include `primitive`, `backward`, `traditional`, `underdeveloped`, etc. among temporalized counterconcepts that historians now distrust, while stressing the paradox that historians cannot cease to temporalize and periodize. Current historical theory therefore asks not only how anthropology made primitive time, but how historical practice itself produces temporal differentiation.

### E. Reassessment of long-range intellectual history
Nico Mouton's 2023 defence of Lovejoy is also relevant. It warns against reducing long-range history of ideas to timeless essences and recovers Lovejoy's insistence that analytically reconstructed relations remain hypotheses requiring historical control. This helps make `primitivism` usable as a long-range object without assuming identity across every occurrence.

### F. Persistence / afterlife rather than simple death
Recent work on sociology (Clayton Fordahl, 2025) argues that `primitive society` became especially important in modernization theory and declined with that theory, while noting contemporary attempts at conceptual revival. The point for historians is not to endorse revival but to avoid a nineteenth-century-only chronology: elements of primitive temporality survive into development/modernization discourse and state classification after formal anthropological abandonment.

## IX. Best full-history periodization for this project

The following sequence is currently stronger than a linear `rise -> critique -> disappearance` story:

1. **Originary / normative primitive** — first, earliest, primitive Church; temporal priority can confer authority.
2. **Comparative recognition / barbarism** — early modern encounters require classificatory vocabularies without yet constituting the mature anthropological primitive.
3. **Stadial conversion** — eighteenth-century histories increasingly turn contemporary cultural difference into evidence for sequential social development.
4. **Modern anthropological consolidation, c. 1860s–1900** — chronological and cultural primitivity become tightly coupled inside evolutionary, imperial, racial, philological, religious, museum, and ethnographic infrastructures.
5. **Critical thickening, c. 1878–1930s** — source criticism, intensive ethnography, functional/relational accounts, and professional specialization restrict what `primitive` alone can explain without immediately eliminating the word.
6. **Second-order historicization, 1920s–1950s** — Lovejoy/Boas and the history-of-ideas programme make `primitivism` itself a historical object while first-order usage continues elsewhere.
7. **Long deauthorization, 1940s–1960s/70s** — decolonization and disciplinary critique progressively strip `primitive` of legitimate actor-neutral status; Hsu/Montagu mark explicit rejection.
8. **Critical genealogy, 1970s–2000s** — historians and anthropologists reconstruct stadialism, allochronism, invented primitive society, empire, museums, classification, and politics of time.
9. **Current differentiating historiography, 2010s–2020s** — preserve the political critique but disaggregate vocabulary, operation, evidence, relation, circulation, institutional jurisdiction, indigenous participation, and version change; also turn the critique of temporalization back onto historians themselves.

## X. Direct payoff for the Huizinga–Malinowski article

The article should not claim that Malinowski `overcame primitive` and Huizinga partly failed to. That would reproduce an older progressive disciplinary story.

The stronger historiographical claim is:

> **Between Huizinga and Malinowski, `primitive` survived while its inferential jurisdiction became increasingly contested.**

Malinowski's ethnography could retain `savage` / `primitive` while shifting explanation into named relations and practices. Huizinga could retain developmental/stadial language while learning to compare feudal obligation, words, gifts, prestige, ceremony, and play more concretely. Their exchange therefore belongs to the long history in which a category remains lexically available while the warrant for letting it settle chronology, function, causality, or civilizational rank is redistributed.

This gives `outsider` a historiographical depth. If `primitive` is no longer a universally self-authorizing bridge, then crossing from history into anthropology increasingly requires a statement about **who may warrant the comparison**. `Benevolent outsider` can therefore be read as a local solution to a wider twentieth-century problem:

`SHARED CATEGORY / SHARED QUESTION`
!=
`SHARED EVIDENTIAL WARRANT`
!=
`SHARED DISCIPLINARY JURISDICTION`.

This is much stronger than treating outsiderhood as modesty or professional boundary alone.

## XI. Historiographical cautions / counter-proofs

Every article paragraph using this history should preserve at least one counter-proof:

- colonial politics != exhaustive explanation of every local inference;
- local evidentiary sophistication != escape from hierarchical macro classification;
- fieldwork != automatic coevalness;
- retention of `primitive` != unchanged evolutionary doctrine;
- abandonment of the word != disappearance of temporal hierarchy;
- comparison != identity of cases;
- analytical similarity != historical derivation;
- shared object != shared warrant;
- shared warrant != shared jurisdiction;
- later critique != proof that actors were uniformly naive about the category.

The desired historiographical position is therefore **post-totalizing but not post-critical**.
