# History and anthropology historiography control — 2026-09-05

## Scope

This note controls a historiographical gap exposed by Draft 04: the article has reconstructed a dense Huizinga–Malinowski exchange but has not yet situated that exchange inside the established historiography of the changing relation between **history and anthropology**.

The bounded questions are:

1. How has the separation and later rapprochement of history and anthropology been periodized?
2. Where do Evans-Pritchard, Keith Thomas, Lévi-Strauss, Peter Burke and later historical-anthropology writers place functionalism and the problem of history?
3. Has Huizinga already been placed inside that history, and if so, how?
4. What does the Huizinga–Malinowski archive add that the existing historiography does not already say?
5. How does this literature change the article's treatment of `primitive`, comparison and `benevolent outsider`?

This is an article-facing historiography control, not a general bibliography of historical anthropology.

Evidence language:

- **PRIMARY / SECURE** — contemporary programmatic text directly controlled.
- **HISTORIOGRAPHY / SECURE** — later historiographical source directly controlled.
- **NOTE** — useful contextual literature whose exact passage is not required for Draft quotation.
- **NEGATIVE CONTROL** — a bounded source does not contain the relation one might otherwise be tempted to infer.

## 1. Governing periodization: not natural separation, but disciplinary differentiation

The strongest broad synthesis for the article is Peter Burke, `Anthropologists and Historians: Reflections on the History of a Relationship`, *Wissenschaftskolleg Jahrbuch 1989/90*, pp. 155–164.

Burke's sequence is:

- eighteenth-century philosophical history had not yet separated comparative social theory from history;
- nineteenth-century professionalization increasingly divided labour among history and the emerging social sciences;
- Rankean history gained critical rigour while narrowing itself around documentary political history;
- fieldwork and functionalism moved social anthropology toward synchronic analysis of present societies and away from conjectural historical reconstruction;
- neither separation was absolute; residual bridges survived;
- from the 1950s–60s onward the disciplines increasingly rediscovered each other, producing what later came to be called `historical anthropology`.

The article-facing point is Burke's symmetry. He argues that the professionalization of anthropology, sociology and neighbouring disciplines required independence from the older discipline of history, and that the rejection of historical method plus a claim to scientific status helped construct new disciplinary identities. He then places Neo-Rankean history and functionalist anthropology side by side: both acquired greater methodological rigour by deliberately excluding problems that did not fit their new professional standards (Burke, 1989/90, pp. 158–160).

This is substantially stronger than a generic `history versus anthropology` contrast. It treats the division as an institutional and methodological achievement rather than as an intrinsic difference between two kinds of knowledge.

Primary locator:
- Peter Burke, `Anthropologists and Historians: Reflections on the History of a Relationship`, *Wissenschaftskolleg Jahrbuch 1989/90*, 155–164, especially pp. 158–163. Open Wiko PDF: `https://www.wiko-berlin.de/fileadmin/Jahrbuchberichte/1989/1989_90_Burke_Peter_Aufsatz_Jahrbuchbericht.pdf`.

### Article consequence

The 1931 Leiden episode should not be described simply as an interdisciplinary encounter. It occurs inside a period when disciplinary difference had become a professional achievement. Malinowski can call Huizinga's historical method `the right sort of really anthropological approach` while simultaneously demanding anthropological personnel, a central principle and a more definite social science. Intellectual resemblance and disciplinary admissibility need not coincide.

That is precisely the historical problem condensed by `benevolent outsider`.

## 2. Burke places Huizinga inside the bridge — but only at high altitude

Burke's synthesis contains a direct Huizinga placement that is unusually important for this project. After describing the disciplinary separation, he insists that historians and social theorists never lost touch completely. His first example is 1919: Huizinga's *Waning of the Middle Ages*, which Burke describes as a study of fourteenth- and fifteenth-century culture drawing on ideas of social anthropologists. He then moves to *Annales*, Schumpeter, Elias and Evans-Pritchard's *The Sanusi of Cyrenaica* before describing the post-1960s stream of cross-disciplinary work.

This gives the project an existing historiographical slot:

> Huizinga is already recognized as one of the residual bridges across the professional separation of history and anthropology.

But Burke's scale leaves the mechanism unspecified. `Huizinga drew on anthropology` and `Malinowski helped separate anthropology from history` appear as two tendencies in the same broad story. The archive in this repo makes those tendencies collide in one sustained relationship.

### Article intervention enabled by the archive

Do **not** claim that Huizinga–Malinowski inaugurated the rapprochement. The stronger and safer intervention is:

> The pair gives a microhistory of the period of maximum disciplinary differentiation. A historian later remembered as a surviving anthropological bridge was in direct, reciprocal contact with one of the canonical builders of fieldwork functionalism. Their exchange shows how disciplinary separation was negotiated in books, judgments, teaching, institutional plans and claims to competence rather than merely imposed at the level of abstract method.

This is more original than precursor language.

## 3. G. W. Locher 1961: the pair was already used to think the history–anthropology problem

A major result of this pass is G. W. Locher, `De antropoloog Lévi-Strauss en het probleem van de geschiedenis`, *Forum der Letteren* 1961, pp. 201–215, available in DBNL.

Locher discusses Lévi-Strauss's distinction between history and anthropology and explicitly places Malinowski within the paradox. He notes that Malinowski opposed `historical anthropology` and neglected much obtainable evidence about the past of societies he studied, yet argues that Malinowski's actual monographic/functional procedure could itself be understood as historical in a methodological sense.

Locher then turns directly to Huizinga. He characterizes Huizinga's *Herfsttij* as predominantly synchronic and morphological rather than a diachronic reconstruction. He writes that Huizinga was fascinated by Malinowski's *Argonauts of the Western Pacific*, a work anthropology regarded as a masterly example of anti-historical functional method. He uses the Huizinga–Malinowski relation to argue that what scholars say they are doing and how they actually work need not coincide.

Immediately afterward Locher contrasts his reading with Evans-Pritchard's freshly published *Anthropology and History* (1961), in which Evans-Pritchard called Radcliffe-Brown and Malinowski `both extremely hostile to history`. Locher accepts the hostility at the level of programmatic statement but refuses to equate it with an absence of historical method.

Locator:
- G. W. Locher, `De antropoloog Lévi-Strauss en het probleem van de geschiedenis`, *Forum der Letteren* 1961, esp. pp. 206–207. DBNL: `https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/_for004196101_01/_for004196101_01_0022.php`.
- Locher cites his own `Huizinga en de culturele antropologie`, *Bijdragen tot de Taal-, Land- en Volkenkunde* 114 (1958), 170–181, esp. 180–181.

### Critical control on Locher

Locher also states that Malinowski's KNAW membership came about through Huizinga's agency. In the current project this remains a **historiographical attribution**, not a controlled reconstruction of nomination mechanics. Draft 04 already preserves the stronger archival fact: Huizinga signed/supports the candidature while explicitly saying `Being no anthropologist I refrained from taking a leading part in your candidature`. Do not let Locher's retrospective formulation overwrite that archive-controlled limit.

### Article consequence

Locher can no longer be used only as the later scholar who said Malinowski did not modify Huizinga's global image of primitive culture. He is also a direct historiographical predecessor for the article's central methodological problem. The present article differs from him by reconstructing the exchange longitudinally and by treating `primitive` as a changing instrument of historical inference rather than deciding whether either scholar was secretly `historical` or `anthropological` in essence.

## 4. Lévi-Strauss: synchrony/structure did not simply equal anti-history

The history–anthropology literature is not exhausted by functionalism versus history. Claude Lévi-Strauss repeatedly made the relation itself a theoretical object.

In `L'Anthropologie sociale devant l'Histoire` (Collège de France inaugural lecture, 5 January 1960; *Annales* 15.4, 625–637), he argues that before one can ask questions of nature or origin, phenomena and their internal relations must first be identified and analysed. This is a decisive complication of any simple `synchronic = anti-historical` story.

Locher's 1961 reading is useful here because it catches the debate contemporaneously. He argues that Lévi-Strauss made the history/anthropology relation sharper than Evans-Pritchard's simple label `hostile to history`, precisely because a monographic-functional analysis could do historical work even while refusing speculative diachrony.

A much later Lévi-Strauss essay, `Histoire et ethnologie` (Annales, 1983), retrospectively begins by emphasizing the unusually close relation between ethnology and history in France and names Marc Bloch's *Les Rois thaumaturges* as an early object capable of attracting ethnologists.

Locators:
- Claude Lévi-Strauss, `L'Anthropologie sociale devant l'Histoire`, *Annales ESC* 15.4 (1960), 625–637, DOI `10.3406/ahess.1960.421640`.
- Claude Lévi-Strauss, `Histoire et ethnologie`, *Annales* (1983), available through Cambridge Core.

### Article consequence

The target is not to rehabilitate Malinowski as `really a historian`. The useful distinction is between:

- rejection of conjectural/evolutionary history;
- exclusion or neglect of archival pasts;
- synchronic reconstruction of relations;
- historical inference from those relations;
- explicit diachronic reconstruction.

The Huizinga–Malinowski case moves among these levels. That is exactly why `primitive` matters: a synchronic ethnographic relation could be made historically portable without requiring the living society to be treated simply as a surviving earlier stage.

## 5. Evans-Pritchard 1950/1961: explicit reopening toward history and the humanities

Evans-Pritchard's Marett Lecture, `Social Anthropology: Past and Present` (*Man* 50, 1950, 118–124), is a primary programmatic hinge. He presents social anthropology as a newly professionalized discipline divided between those who treat it as a natural science and those who locate it among the humanities. His argument pushes against a purely natural-science conception and insists that anthropology cannot discuss social change while excluding events and history.

His 1961 Manchester lecture *Anthropology and History* makes the rapprochement explicit. It rejects an easy opposition between a generalizing anthropology and a particularizing history. Evans-Pritchard argues in practice for sociological handling of historical material and for anthropology's serious engagement with documentary and oral evidence. The lecture became an immediate point of cross-disciplinary discussion.

Locators:
- E. E. Evans-Pritchard, `Social Anthropology: Past and Present`, Marett Lecture 1950, *Man* 50 (1950), 118–124.
- E. E. Evans-Pritchard, *Anthropology and History: A Lecture Delivered in the University of Manchester with the Support of the Simon Fund for the Social Sciences* (Manchester University Press, 1961), 22 pp.

Modern historiography warns against turning this into a sudden conversion. Joel Isaac (2022) dates anthropology's turn to history as early as the 1950s but argues that the precise character of that turn has become obscured by disciplinary folklore. He explains the attraction of history partly through the problem of the origins and persistence of institutions and explicitly reconstructs a line through Durkheim, Malinowski and Geertz.

Locator:
- Joel Isaac, `Anthropology and the Turn to History`, in Richard Bourke and Quentin Skinner (eds), *History in the Humanities and Social Sciences* (Cambridge University Press, 2022), 379–407, DOI `10.1017/9781009231053.017`.

### Article consequence

Do not make Evans-Pritchard the moment at which anthropologists suddenly discovered history. He is better used as the moment when a previously contested relation became explicit disciplinary programme. Our case sits one generation earlier and shows that the problem was already live in practice before it became a canonical methodological manifesto.

## 6. 1961–63: rapprochement becomes an explicit cross-disciplinary conversation

Keith Thomas is essential because Evans-Pritchard's intervention immediately moved across the disciplinary boundary.

Thomas's `Should Historians Be Anthropologists?`, *Oxford Magazine*, 1 June 1961, responded to Evans-Pritchard. His expanded `History and Anthropology`, *Past & Present* 24 (April 1963), 3–24, argues that British historians could profit from anthropology and presents the recent anti-historical/natural-science orientation of British social anthropology as itself changing.

Freddy Foks's 2024 reconstruction confirms the chain: Thomas wrote the 1961 article in response to Evans-Pritchard's 1950 and 1961 remarks; Lawrence Stone then invited him to present the argument at a seminar; a revised version became the 1963 *Past & Present* article.

Julian Pitt-Rivers's review essay `History and Anthropology`, *Comparative Studies in Society and History* 5.2 (January 1963), 253–258, shows that this was already a recognizable conversation. His references place Evans-Pritchard's 1961 lecture and Thomas's 1961 Oxford Magazine piece side by side.

Locators:
- Keith Thomas, `Should Historians Be Anthropologists?`, *Oxford Magazine*, 1 June 1961, 387–388.
- Keith Thomas, `History and Anthropology`, *Past & Present* 24 (1963), 3–24, DOI `10.1093/past/24.1.3`.
- Julian Pitt-Rivers, `History and Anthropology`, *Comparative Studies in Society and History* 5.2 (1963), 253–258, DOI `10.1017/S001041750000164X`.
- Freddy Foks, `Finding modernity in England's past: Social anthropology and the remaking of social history in Britain, 1959–77`, *History of the Human Sciences* 37.3–4 (2024), 106–129, DOI `10.1177/09526951241242327`.

### Important direction of influence

The postwar rapprochement did not initially require anthropologists to become historians first. Historians often borrowed precisely the ethnographic and functional tools that anthropology was beginning to question. Burke later captures this with his `two trains on parallel lines` image: historians discovered functional explanations in the 1960s while anthropologists were becoming dissatisfied with them.

This matters for Draft 04 because it blocks a teleology in which functionalism first collapses and then history enters. The traffic was asynchronous.

## 7. 1959–77 British social history: anthropology supplied the ethnographic present as a model for the past

Foks (2024) gives the strongest recent reconstruction of what historians actually did with anthropology. Hobsbawm's *Primitive Rebels*, Laslett's family history, Keith Thomas's witchcraft work and E. P. Thompson's moral economy all drew on contemporary social anthropology in different ways.

Especially important for this article:

- Hobsbawm's *Primitive Rebels* arose partly from an invitation by Max Gluckman and Meyer Fortes to consider European precedents for African anti-colonial rebellions. Anthropology and history were linked through developmental and modernization questions, not only through benign interdisciplinary borrowing.
- E. P. Thompson explicitly invoked Malinowski and the Trobriand Islanders in the moral-economy argument to resist reducing eighteenth-century market actors to abstract economic man. Kula/reciprocity became a resource for historical description of non-capitalist exchange.
- Thompson then warned against removing anthropological findings from their context and treating them as typological facts valid everywhere.

This is an important later analogue to the problem already present in the Huizinga–Malinowski exchange: when may an ethnographically described relation travel historically without turning a living society into a timeless type?

Foks does not mention Huizinga. That bounded absence is useful but should not be inflated into a field-wide claim. It shows that a current account of the British 1959–77 rapprochement can be written through contemporary social anthropology without recovering the earlier Huizinga bridge.

## 8. 1968: history enters the ASA agenda, but integration remains a problem

I. M. Lewis's edited ASA volume *History and Social Anthropology* (1968) is an institutional landmark. Its stated purpose is to examine how social anthropologists might gain from and contribute to historical studies. The case chapters use documentary, linguistic and political-historical material from Africa, Europe and elsewhere.

The volume is useful not because it solves the relation but because it shows that by the late 1960s `history and social anthropology` had become a collective professional agenda inside British anthropology.

Locator:
- I. M. Lewis (ed.), *History and Social Anthropology*, ASA Monographs 7 (London: Tavistock, 1968), xxviii + 307 pp.

Later historiography has noted that the conjunction remained partly additive: history could mean a longer temporal record appended to social-anthropological analysis rather than a full rethinking of anthropological objects and causation. Keep that distinction in mind when using `historical turn` language.

## 9. Cohn 1980: from disciplinary rapprochement to knowledge practices

Bernard S. Cohn's `History and Anthropology: The State of Play`, *Comparative Studies in Society and History* 22.2 (1980), 198–221, changes the level of the debate. Cohn describes two idealized disciplinary worlds, `Anthropologyland` and `Historyland`, and asks how each produces, writes and speaks knowledge.

This is particularly useful for Draft 04 because our article already has a material-method contrast:

- Malinowski: repeated field observation, language, living interlocutors, relational reconstruction;
- Huizinga: notes/cards, surviving texts and objects, copies, photographs, transmission chains and correction.

Cohn supplies historiographical warrant for treating that difference as a history of disciplinary knowledge practices rather than as a generic opposition between `present` and `past`.

Locator:
- Bernard S. Cohn, `History and Anthropology: The State of Play`, *Comparative Studies in Society and History* 22.2 (1980), 198–221, DOI `10.1017/S0010417500009294`.

## 10. 1980s historical anthropology: structure/event, connected histories, and temporal politics

By the early 1980s the problem had moved beyond whether history and anthropology should cooperate.

### Marshall Sahlins

*Historical Metaphors and Mythical Realities* (1981) uses Hawaiian encounters with foreign traders and settlers to develop a structuralist methodology of historical interpretation. The problem becomes how cultural structures are reproduced and transformed in events.

Locator:
- Marshall D. Sahlins, *Historical Metaphors and Mythical Realities: Structure in the Early History of the Sandwich Islands Kingdom* (University of Michigan Press, 1981).

### Eric Wolf

*Europe and the People Without History* (1982) attacks the isolation of supposedly bounded, static non-European societies and reconstructs them inside connected processes of European expansion and global political economy. History is not simply added to ethnographic societies; the bounded anthropological object itself is destabilized.

Locator:
- Eric R. Wolf, *Europe and the People Without History* (University of California Press, 1982).

### Johannes Fabian

*Time and the Other* (1983) is the most direct later historiographical/theoretical control on this project's `primitive` argument. Fabian attacks anthropology's denial of coevalness: anthropologist and subject may interact in shared time during fieldwork while anthropological discourse relocates the subject into a `there and then`. His history of anthropological uses of Time makes temporal distancing a constitutive epistemic operation rather than a stray vocabulary problem.

Locator:
- Johannes Fabian, *Time and the Other: How Anthropology Makes Its Object* (Columbia University Press, 1983; later editions 2002/2014).

### Article consequence

Fabian should remain a downstream diagnostic, not be projected backward as the actors' own concept. The article's archival contribution is to show a more local, unstable mechanism by which present difference could become historical anteriority and by which that inference increasingly required specification through relations, evidence and disciplinary authority.

## 11. Later historical anthropology and the archive

The later literature increasingly treats history/anthropology not as two finished disciplines to be added together but as different regimes of evidence, temporality, power and representation.

Relevant later markers include:

- John and Jean Comaroff, *Ethnography and the Historical Imagination* (1992), integrating ethnography, colonial history, power and historical agency;
- historical-anthropological work associated with Natalie Zemon Davis, Carlo Ginzburg, Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie, Jacques Le Goff, Alan Macfarlane and others;
- William G. Pooley's `Native to the Past: History, Anthropology, and Folklore in Past and Present` (2018), which reconstructs the long place of anthropological history in *Past & Present* and links Thomas, Thompson, Davis, mentalités, popular culture and narrative;
- Herman Roodenburg, *Anthropologists, Historians and the Pulse of the Archive* (2009/2010), which retrospectively describes the two disciplines as a long `pas de deux` and explicitly lists Bloch, Febvre and **Huizinga** among possible earlier historical overtures before dating the more explicit entrée to Thomas's encounter with Evans-Pritchard in spring 1961.

Locators:
- William G. Pooley, `Native to the Past: History, Anthropology, and Folklore in Past and Present`, *Past & Present* 239.1 (2018), e1–e15, DOI `10.1093/pastj/gtv038` (with 2018 corrigendum).
- Herman Roodenburg, *Antropologen, historici en de hartslag van het archief* (Amsterdam: Vrije Universiteit, 2009); English bibliographic record as *Anthropologists, Historians and the Pulse of the Archive* (VU University Press, 2010).

Roodenburg's formulation is especially useful because it distinguishes **earlier overtures** from the later explicit disciplinary `entrée`. That language fits the present project's chronology better than `precursor`.

## 12. Huizinga-specific historiography: bridge, anthropologist avant la lettre, and philological control

The broader history–anthropology literature intersects with the existing Huizinga literature in three different ways.

### 12.1 Burke: Huizinga as a surviving bridge

Burke's 1989/90 essay is the cleanest placement of Huizinga inside the history of the disciplinary relation itself: *Waning* is one of the works showing that contact never fully disappeared.

### 12.2 Arnade and later `anthropologist avant la lettre` readings

Peter Arnade, `Huizinga: Anthropologist Avant la Lettre?` in *Rereading Huizinga* (2019), makes the anthropological affinities of Huizinga's cultural history explicit. This is useful for identifying what later readers recognize as anthropological in his treatment of ritual, cultural form and comparison.

### 12.3 Philological/linguistic caution

Recent Huizinga scholarship also warns that reading him as an early anthropologist can flatten his philological and linguistic formation. The *English Historical Review* centenary review of the recent Huizinga literature explicitly notes that contributors can be too eager to make Huizinga an early exponent of anthropological/ethnological cultural history while underplaying his formation in philology, etymology and linguistics.

This caution agrees with the article's existing Arnade ↔ Vale control: anthropology changed questions and comparative materials more readily than it replaced Huizinga's historical/philological source practices.

### Article consequence

Avoid both extremes:

- `Huizinga was already an anthropologist, therefore outsiderhood was artificial`;
- `Huizinga was a historian and Malinowski an anthropologist, therefore their methods were essentially separate`.

The archive shows proximity and non-identity at once.

## 13. Revised historiographical model for Draft 04

The literature supports the following controlled sequence:

### A. Long nineteenth century: common ancestry → professional separation

Comparative history, ethnology and social theory share problems of social difference, development and institutions. Professionalization then makes disciplinary boundaries more explicit.

### B. c.1900–1940: maximum differentiation, but residual bridges

Neo-Rankean historical standards narrow one side; fieldwork and functionalist standards narrow another. Yet Huizinga, Bloch/Febvre and other cultural/social historians keep anthropological questions in historical work. Burke later calls Huizinga one of the examples showing contact never disappeared.

**This is where the article lives.**

### C. 1950–63: relation becomes explicit disciplinary programme

Evans-Pritchard attacks the natural-science closure and calls anthropology toward history/humanities; Keith Thomas immediately asks what historians can learn from anthropology; Pitt-Rivers treats the exchange as an identifiable debate.

### D. 1968–80: collaboration becomes institutional and methodological problem

ASA's *History and Social Anthropology* makes the relation a collective programme. Cohn shifts attention to how the two disciplines create and handle evidence.

### E. 1980s onward: historical anthropology transforms the object

Sahlins tackles structure/event; Wolf attacks isolated peoples and reconnects them to world history; Fabian attacks temporal distancing; later historical anthropology brings colonialism, power, archive and agency into the relation.

## 14. The strongest article-level intervention

The historiography now makes one intervention especially defensible:

> **Huizinga and Malinowski should be treated neither as founders of a later history–anthropology rapprochement nor as representatives of two naturally opposed disciplines. Their 1926–33 exchange is a microhistory of disciplinary differentiation in action.**

Burke tells us that Huizinga belongs among the residual bridges and that functionalist anthropology belongs among the forces of professional narrowing. Locher, already in 1961, recognized that the Huizinga–Malinowski relation exposed a mismatch between declared disciplinary method and actual scholarly practice. What the current archive adds is the sequence of transactions by which that mismatch was negotiated:

`seminar / conversation`
→ `books sent and read`
→ `historical use tested against ethnographic relations`
→ `Huizinga's modern-comparative writing returned to Malinowski for judgment`
→ `Trobriand familiarity coexisting with stadial language`
→ `institutional programme audited by Malinowski`
→ `historical method praised as really anthropological`
→ `disciplinary authority nonetheless withheld/self-limited`
→ `Kula relation carried into a 1933 historical comparison`.

This is not a story in which disciplines dissolve. It is a history of **what could cross the boundary before authority did**.

## 15. Relation to `primitive`

The history–anthropology literature also sharpens the article's conceptual center.

Earlier comparative anthropology often made living difference do historical work by arranging peoples and institutions along developmental or evolutionary sequences. Functionalist fieldwork attacked speculative historical reconstruction by demanding relational analysis in the present, but did not thereby abolish `primitive` as a classification. Postwar historical rapprochement then had to recover temporality without simply returning to conjectural evolution.

This supplies a larger disciplinary context for the article's `waning` claim:

`FORM PERSISTS + INFERENTIAL SUFFICIENCY WANES + EXPLANATORY WORK IS REDISTRIBUTED`.

Fabian shows the later critical endpoint: even after evolutionism's decline, anthropology could continue to deny coevalness through temporalized representation. The Huizinga–Malinowski archive catches an earlier unstable stage in which a living ethnographic relation could be carried into history while the old category and stadial language still survived.

## 16. Draft-use rule

This historiography materially changes framing, but **do not patch Draft 04 by dumping a literature-review section into it**.

Likely later surgical uses are:

1. **Introduction / historiographical positioning:** one compact paragraph locating the pair in the professional separation described by Burke and distinguishing the article from a precursor narrative.
2. **1931 institution section:** Burke's professionalization argument can frame why `really anthropological` and `benevolent outsider` are compatible rather than contradictory.
3. **Historiographical note / Locher:** expand Locher's job from counterevidence about `primitive` to direct predecessor on the history–anthropology problem, while preserving the KNAW mechanics caution.
4. **Conclusion / later control:** Evans-Pritchard–Thomas can show that what remained an unstable interwar crossing became explicit disciplinary programme after 1950, without claiming a direct genealogy from Huizinga.
5. **Fabian:** retain as downstream analytic historiography for temporal distancing, not actor vocabulary.

No Draft 04 patch is authorized by this note alone.
