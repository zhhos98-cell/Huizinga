## p50–51 — opening/proem draft

Huizinga opens from the circumstance of speaking in Paris/Sorbonne, the place he associates with the birth/formation of scholastic culture. He apologises for speaking French as a foreigner and defines the intended lecture as historical rather than technical-philosophical.

On p51 the planned subject `Abailard comme type de son époque` is explicit. Huizinga says that simply announcing Abélard might lead the audience to expect a lecture on medieval philosophy; that is precisely what he does not claim to offer. His task is to connect the life/character of persons with the civilisation of their epoch.

## p52 — bibliographical insert

p52 is not continuous lecture prose. It is a compact reference card containing bibliographical controls, including Savonarola and Marsilio Ficino material. It belongs to the working dossier but should not be inserted into the Paris lecture sequence as prose.

## p54–56 — three `esprits` and the heuristic `prégothique`

The fuller lecture version begins at p54. Huizinga names the three selected figures:

- Abélard / `Abailard`;
- Jean de Salisbury;
- Alain de Lille.

He calls them `esprits prégothiques`, but immediately treats the label as a heuristic rather than a rigid period/style category. p55–56 explicitly resist loading `gothique` with an absolute philosophical meaning. The practical value of the term is to make perceptible relations among forms taken by thought, architecture, social life and expression in the same historical formation.

France, and especially Paris, becomes the privileged setting for this twelfth-century problem.

## p57–59 — twelfth century as formation / fermentation

p57 contains the programmatic claim that, if one had to choose the time when Western Christian civilisation acquired a decisive form, Huizinga would choose the **twelfth century**. He calls it an exceptionally creative/formative epoch.

p58 inventories new or newly intensified forms: ogival/pointed construction, stained glass, tournament, communal liberties, new mysticism (Saint Bernard / Hugh of Saint Victor), scholasticism, universities, intensified religious expression, wandering students/goliards and heterodox movements.

p59 gives the key formulation:

`Epoque de formation vaut dire époque de fermentation.`

The historical content of `fermentation` is mobility and unstable circulation: pilgrims, crusaders, preachers, knights seeking adventure, students, jongleurs, merchants, brigands and migrants/colonists. The world becomes more mobile, more crowded with contacts and more coloured both materially and spiritually.

## p60–62 — Haskins and `The Renaissance of the Twelfth Century`

p60 explicitly introduces C. H. Haskins and *The Renaissance of the Twelfth Century*. Huizinga accepts the richness of the phenomena Haskins assembled: Latin poetry/classicism, schools, goliardic production, translation from Arabic and Greek, new science/philosophy and the enlarged presence of Aristotle.

The question is terminological and causal. By p62 Huizinga asks whether Haskins has assigned **too much causal weight to the revival of the classical element** in explaining the development of Christian medieval civilisation.

This is not a rejection of classical transmission. Aristotle in particular is acknowledged as enormously consequential. The objection concerns explanatory hierarchy.

## p63–66 — alternative causal architecture

Huizinga identifies deeper or at least equally fundamental forces:

- Gregorian/ecclesiastical reform and the idea/primacy of the Church;
- monastic reforms and orders, including Cluny/Cîteaux and related formations;
- crusading/religious orders;
- chivalry;
- cooperative/corporate forms;
- towns and schools;
- political organisms;
- economic and communal organisation.

p64 reframes the relation to antiquity over the longue durée. Medieval culture repeatedly had to **liquidate, adapt or transform** an inherited classical treasure: Augustine, late antique/Merovingian survivals and the Carolingian revival are moments in that continuing relation rather than discrete births of civilisation.

p65 is especially clear that the formation of medieval civilisation proceeds through the forms taken by social life — monasticism, feudality, political organisms and corporate/common economic organisation — and that these phenomena did not principally arise from classical imitation.

p66 therefore presents twelfth-century Europe as an already maturing organism: Church/canon law, Empire, crusade, feudal/chivalric forms, Norman monarchy, towns and schools are in place when antiquity is encountered with new intensity.

## p67 — `naissance, non pas renaissance`

This page contains the conceptual payoff. Antiquity acquires a new meaning for a vigorous twelfth-century civilisation, but Huizinga refuses to make the classical element itself the thing that is reborn.

The secure argument is:

- what is born is a **medieval Christian civilisation**;
- classical material stimulates it and is absorbed by it;
- the classical *form* cannot simply be said to return unchanged;
- therefore the better description is **`naissance, non pas renaissance`**.

This is one of the quotation-sensitive pages of the dossier and should be cited from the image, not from raw OCR.

## p68–69 — from twelfth-century agitation to thirteenth-century order

Huizinga closes the framing section by making `formation` a crisis process: agitation, experimentation and mental excess allow forms to run beyond the restraints that later systems will impose.

The thirteenth century is then characterised by **order, harmony and system**. Scholasticism supplies intellectual cadres; the Church is described not only as an organ of faith but as the exceptional institution capable of gathering, ordering and transmitting the elements required for a civilisation across a mass society.

This is the bridge to Abélard: the twelfth-century condition is productive precisely because its intellectual/social energies have not yet been fully disciplined by the later system.

## Result

The Paris 1930 introduction is now text-controlled as a coherent argument:

1. `prégothique` is a heuristic relation among cultural forms, not a rigid style label;
2. the twelfth century is `formation/fermentation`;
3. Haskins correctly identifies major classical/intellectual revivals but, for Huizinga, does not exhaust the causal field;
4. institutional/social forms are constitutive of civilisation;
5. the decisive formula is `naissance, non pas renaissance`;
6. twelfth-century ferment moves toward thirteenth-century order/system.
