## p179 — Berengar's satire and Bernard's rhetoric

This is one of the strongest cards in the packet. Secure material includes:

- a Latin passage/quotation field mocking Bernard through bodily/fatness imagery (`pinguedo`/related vocabulary visible);
- a clear line field `Petrus semper turbat Ecclesiam, semper excogitat novitatem ...` in the copied polemical material;
- Huizinga's Dutch comments on **parody**, Bernard's position and the style of Berengar;
- repeated cross-references to `AB. 2.772`, `2.774`, `2.786/7` and other edition controls.

Huizinga is not simply collecting doctrinal propositions: he is controlling tone, invective and rhetorical style as historical evidence.

## p180 — Berengar of Poitiers as stylistic/polemical witness

The right-hand sheet explicitly names `Berengarius de Poiteau` / Berengar of Poitiers and continues edition references around `AB. 2.771`, `2.779` etc.

Huizinga comments on Bernard's **rhetoric**, describing the polemic as sharp/ironic and asking how Berengar's literary habit relates to broader contemporary groups (vagantes/courtly or learned styles are part of the comparison field).

This page is therefore best tagged `polemical style / rhetoric`, not simply `Abélard doctrine`.

## p181 — literary form, court/Rome and Bernard

The mounted cards continue quotation controls and contrast literary/artistic capacity with polemical judgement. Secure fields include:

The sheet is important for the same reason as p179–180: controversy is being analysed through **form of expression and institutional location** as much as theological content.

## Archival interpretation

p176–183 is a coherent research packet distinct from the Paris 1930 lecture. Its main layers are:

1. Abélard/Bernard comparison;
2. Berengar of Poitiers as polemical witness;
3. edition/page concordance and Latin quotation harvesting;
4. rhetoric, irony, parody and invective as evidence;
5. institutional fields such as Church/curia and controversy;
6. a closing concordance/index.

This material is especially reusable for studying how Huizinga converted controversy into a comparative history of intellectual **types and styles**, rather than merely collecting doctrinal positions.
