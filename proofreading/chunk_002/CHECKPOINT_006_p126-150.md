### p126–136 — end of Jean de Salisbury

The Jean section continues from p125 into political and social thought.

- p126 opens from the `Policraticus`, especially the contrast between the courtier/`curialis` and the philosopher as competing principles of life.
- p127–130 develop Huizinga's claim that Jean's outlook is simultaneously critical, humane, ecclesiastical and sociological. Community, social belonging and the changing judgement of office-holders as they rise through institutional ranks are central examples.
- p131–133 move to prince, law, public utility, equity, foreign peoples / `ius gentium`-type concerns, political vice, tyranny and tyrannicide. Huizinga treats the prince as servant of law/public utility rather than an autonomous source of law.
- p134–136 return from formal political doctrine to court culture. `Nugae curialium`, courtiers, hypocrites, actors/histriones and worldly play are used to define what Jean rejects. Huizinga ends by construing Jean as an early/hybrid humanistic type whose ideal of learned ecclesiastical seriousness confronts courtly culture before later social/intellectual forms have fully hardened.

`p136` is the last continuous Jean de Salisbury manuscript page in this sequence.

### p144 — third `esprit` begins: Alain de Lille

The transition is explicit. Huizinga first recapitulates his method:

- Abélard was used primarily for the intellectual side of the `prégothique` condition;
- Jean de Salisbury for character/personality and social-cultural type;
- **Alain de Lille** will be used to develop views on the aesthetic side of twelfth-century civilisation, especially imagination / `fantaisie` and poetic expression.

Huizinga immediately asks whether Alain is still legitimately `prégothique`, since some features seem to anticipate later epochs.

### p145–150 — Alain: biography, legend, and the problem of images

- p145–147: uncertain biography, extraordinary medieval reputation, legends, *Anticlaudianus* and *De planctu Naturae*; Huizinga begins from Alain's extraordinary verbal/poetic artifices rather than systematic theology.
- p148–150: the argument turns into a media-history problem. Huizinga asks the audience to imagine a world with a relative **poverty of visible images** before the later Gothic saturation of churches and before modern graphic/photographic abundance. He proposes that verbal imagery and imagination have an unusually strong cultural function under such conditions.
- p149 explicitly links image scarcity to the extraordinary efficacy of both spoken word and newly created image; twelfth-century civilisation is described as still living heavily through the word while plastic/visual expression is only beginning its great expansion.
- p150 adds an important qualification: poverty of images does **not** mean poverty of colour. Huizinga evokes a strongly polychrome twelfth-century world of dyed surfaces, fabrics, gems, pigments and the coming stained glass of cathedrals.

## Research significance

The third figure makes the architecture of the Paris 1930 lecture much more precise. `Prégothique` is not being applied to three biographies in parallel. Huizinga distributes analytical functions across figures:

1. Abélard — intellectual/dialectical formation and institutional ecology of learning;
2. Jean de Salisbury — social, moral, political and humanistic type;
3. Alain de Lille — imagination, verbal image, colour, symbolism and aesthetic formation.

The lecture is therefore a composite analysis of a civilisation through differently chosen persons.
