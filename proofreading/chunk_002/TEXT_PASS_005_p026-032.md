## p26 — physical PDF p26 / `chunk_002:p0026`

Huizinga continues the political history begun on p25. The page moves from the old Tuscan city autonomies into the internal organisation of Florence.

Secure sequence:

- Matilda's legacy and the weakening/withdrawal of imperial authority are used to frame the rise of Tuscan urban autonomy; Pisa, Siena, Lucca and Florence are named together.
- Florence's relation to imperial authority under Frederick Barbarossa and Henry VI is noted.
- `adel in de stad` and the opposition/alignment of nobles and burghers are treated as a constitutional problem rather than a simple social contrast.
- by the mid-thirteenth century Huizinga tracks the growing institutional weight of the `popolo` beside the `podestà` and `comune`.
- he notes a `capitano del popolo`, a council/college of `12 oudsten (anziani)` and the companies/organisations through which the burghers could be mobilised.
- the Guelf/Ghibelline conflict is tied to the Tuscan inter-city system: Pisa, Siena, Pistoia and Arezzo recur as political/military counterparts.
- `Montaperti` (1260) is the decisive military catastrophe; the Florentine Guelf army is defeated by Siena and the imperial/Ghibelline side.
- `Farinata degli Uberti` is singled out in the aftermath; Huizinga then follows the return of Guelf power and the reorganisation after Charles of Anjou.
- the page closes with the increasingly complicated relation among `popolo`, old institutions, councils and constitutional offices.

The page is therefore not a chronology alone. Huizinga is tracking how conflict changes the institutional architecture of the city.

## p27 — physical PDF p27 / `chunk_002:p0027`

The page is headed by the transition to the social-political organisation of the `popolo`, especially the guild system.

### Guild structure

Huizinga distinguishes:

- the `7 groote gilden` / `arti maggiori`;
- the `14 kleine` guilds.

The major guilds are listed in compressed form; secure items include merchants/notaries/judges, `cambio` (banking/exchange), wool, apothecaries, silk, and fur-related trades. The lesser guilds are represented by shopkeepers and craft groups such as butchers, shoemakers, builders, smiths and locksmiths.

### Constitutional consequence

Huizinga then links guild membership to political qualification:

- 1282: government by priors drawn from the seven major guilds;
- 1293: `Ordinamenti di Giustizia` associated with `Giano della Bella`;
- magnates/grandi are excluded from political office unless absorbed into the guild structure;
- the three rectors are written as `podestà`, `capitano` and `gonfaloniere della giustizia`;
- the gonfaloniere is described as having an armed/guild mobilisation function against violence and magnate power.

Huizinga explicitly notes that this does not make the old nobility disappear. Instead nobles adapt to bourgeois/guild life, while a new `popolo grasso` / patriciate forms from combinations of wealth, landed position, old noble style and mercantile power.

Names/families in this social field include `Rucellai`, `Tornabuoni`, `Peruzzi`, `Pulci`, and `Acciaiuoli` (some surrounding spellings still need authority control).

The page ends by reconnecting this social order to faction: `Neri - Bianchi`, `Corso Donati`, and the victory over Arezzo/Ghibellines at `Campaldino` (1289).

## p28 — physical PDF p28 / `chunk_002:p0028`

This page makes the economic dimension explicit.

### Currency, commerce, credit

Huizinga notes:

- Florence's place in the Tuscan league/bond;
- the `fiorino` / florin as the city's great gold coin;
- a c.1300 scale statement of roughly `400.000` (the unit/precise statistic still requires source verification);
- Florentine merchants and financial agents active outside Tuscany, including England/London and Rome;
- the northern label `Lombarden` for Italian moneylenders/financiers.

`Villani` is repeatedly cited as source control.

The central family/example is `Peruzzi`. Huizinga follows the Peruzzi as bankers/financiers, deposits and public/private credit, and notes their later collapse in connection with English royal debt. The exact monetary figures are left unnormalised pending bibliographical/source verification.

### Moral vocabulary and faction

A striking transition then moves from economics to Dante's language:

> `ardor habendi`

and Dante's vocabulary of

> `cieca cupidigia`

with the phrase

> `gente nuova ed i subiti guadagni`

The economic expansion of Florence is thus recorded together with a contemporary moral diagnosis of greed, new wealth and rapid gain.

The lower part returns to `Cerchi`/Bianchi and `Donati`/Neri, Boniface VIII and papal intervention, preparing the Charles of Valois episode on p29.

## p29 — physical PDF p29 / `chunk_002:p0029`

The page begins with the Bianchi/Neri crisis and the entry of `Karel v. Valois` / Charles of Valois into Florence in 1301.

Secure points:

- Charles enters as a supposed mediator/peace-maker but power shifts to the Neri;
- prisons are opened and political opponents are expelled;
- the 1302 banishments include `Dante`;
- Huizinga links the episode to Boniface VIII and to the wider question of monarchy/papal political intervention.

### Trecento constitutional comparison

A major analytical move follows. Huizinga compares Florence with other Italian cities where `podestà` or vicariate develops into `signoria`:

- Milan — Visconti;
- Ferrara — Este;
- Ravenna — Polenta;
- Rimini — Malatesta;
- Verona — Scala/Scaligeri;
- Genoa and Venice are invoked as different oligarchic/constitutional trajectories.

Florence is then treated as a different case: repeated experiments with broadening or narrowing participation rather than a stable hereditary signoria.

`Gautier de Brienne, il duca d'Atene` appears as the major failed signorial episode; Huizinga records his brief rule and expulsion, after which constitutional balancing among major and lesser guild/popolo groups resumes.

The lower lines introduce the rise of mercenary `compagnie` / `condottieri`, including `Werner v. Urslingen` and `John Hawkwood`, and Huizinga comments that the citizenry loses part of its former martial character as war becomes a profession/business.

## p31 — physical PDF p31; architecture text incorrectly merged into OCR p30

This is the real beginning of the architectural/urban-form section.

Huizinga opens with a programmatic question about the relation between Florence's city history and its monumental building campaigns. The secure formulation is approximately:

> `Florence maakt ... Samenhang met stadgesch. - zeker.`

He notes that major building works follow major political developments, then gives a sequence including:

- older parts of the `Pal. del podestà`;
- `Ponte S. Trinità`;
- communal/public building and road/paving programmes;
- 1290s projects involving `S. Maria del Fiore` and the `Pal. della Signoria`.

The analytical question is explicit: republican building activity should not simply be read as civic humility. Huizinga stresses pride, monumentality and collective self-representation.

A crucial line connects Florence's physical city to civic self-consciousness:

> `de inmenging aan de schoone stad`

and the famous praise `Godi Fiorenza...` is cited in the same field.

### Romanesque/material form

Huizinga then turns to formal vocabulary:

- Romanesque construction and the relation between construction and material;
- restrained structural ornament such as `boogfriezen`, colonnades, capitals and portals;
- survival of mosaic from Byzantine tradition;
- Tuscan use of coloured stone/marble and inlay;
- named stone/material references include Carrara marble and `verde di Prato`.

This page is therefore the bridge from institutional history to built form: political history does not stop when architecture begins.

### Right side — Baptistery and Gothic/Italian architecture

Huizinga turns to the Florentine Baptistery (`Bapt. S. Giovanni`) and architectural chronology, noting medieval rebuilding/decoration phases and the old/classical character of elements such as capitals.

He then formulates the Gothic principle comparatively:

- concentration of load on piers/columns;
- `het verticale`;
- reduced wall-mass;
- sculpture moving toward capitals/portals/exterior articulation;
- stained glass/interior effects.

For Italy he stresses a different balance: stronger horizontal/formal restraint, less complete adoption of northern Gothic structural logic, and continued space for surface decoration, inlay and fresco.

He names `S. Croce`, `Arnolfo di Cambio`, `S. Maria Novella`, and the projected Florence cathedral/duomo in the continuation.
