## p126 — `Policraticus`

Huizinga opens from John of Salisbury's *Policraticus*, giving/recognising the subtitle **`de nugis curialium et vestigiis philosophorum`**. He treats the book as more than a government manual. Its structure opposes two principles/forms of life:

- the `curia` / courtier / man of court;
- the philosopher.

That opposition will control the rest of the section.

## p127 — an `Erasmian` comparison

Huizinga explicitly returns to a comparison with Erasmus. The point is a family resemblance in intellectual temperament rather than identity of doctrine: critical and humane, ecclesiastical and pious, serious but capable of lightness/irony, and able to use figures from antiquity to judge the present.

Huizinga then marks the difference: John is an active church/political agent, much more directly involved in affairs than the later scholar-humanist comparison might suggest.

## p128–130 — social belonging and sociological observation

Huizinga increasingly describes John's thought in **social** rather than narrowly constitutional terms.

Secure themes:

- contempt for servility/slavery as contrary to human dignity;
- civilisation / `civilitas` and the social bond;
- happiness cannot be imagined outside community/society;
- actions that strengthen human association belong to the route toward the good;
- an anecdote about an ecclesiastical office-holder changing his judgement of superiors/equal/subordinates as he himself rises through ranks is read by Huizinga as evidence of an unusually sharp **sociological instinct**;
- Huizinga explicitly draws attention to `sentiment de groupe` / group feeling as an explanatory factor.

These pages are important because Huizinga is not simply extracting medieval political doctrine; he is reading John as an observer of institutional behaviour.

## p131–133 — prince, law, public utility, tyranny

Huizinga then turns to formal political doctrine.

Secure propositions include:

- the prince is bound to/serves law rather than standing as its arbitrary source;
- government is oriented to the people and **public utility**;
- the ruler is presented as servant of equity and in a subordinate/service relation to ecclesiastical authority;
- duties toward foreign peoples are noted as containing an early germ of a law-of-peoples / `droit des gens` type idea;
- John's critique of contemporary English royal government is often displaced into moral/classical categories and personified vices rather than direct naming;
- the section reaches the problem of **tyranny and tyrannicide**, followed by Thomas Becket and the language of liberty.

Latin quotations on p133 remain visible but should be checked word-for-word before publication quotation; the page-level argument is secure.

## p134–135 — `nugae curialium`, courtiers, hypocrites and histriones

Huizinga leaves constitutional theory and returns to the cultural meaning of the title *De nugis curialium*.

For John, `nugae` are treated as the opposite of philosophical seriousness: courtly distractions, worldly play, frivolity and forms of conduct unworthy of the serious learned/ecclesiastical person.

The hostile categories widen to:

- courtiers;
- hypocrites;
- actors / `histriones`;
- the preference of society for entertainment and songs/love performance over grave intellectual-moral concerns.

Huizinga is trying to determine whether these condemnations reveal a more general conflict between two cultural ideals rather than a list of personal dislikes.

## p136 — synthesis of the second `esprit`

The closing page gives the conceptual synthesis.

Huizinga sees John as a precocious/hybrid humanistic type:

- deeply ecclesiastical and Christian;
- saturated in the available Latin antiquity;
- committed to a cultivated life of letters, judgement and service;
- nevertheless situated before later social/intellectual boundaries have hardened.

The attack on `curial`, `nugae`, `hypocrite`, `histrion` is therefore read as resistance to a competing **courtly culture/form of life**. Huizinga ends by placing John at a historical fork: later medieval culture will be reshaped both by stronger scholastic/systematic forms and by renewed monastic/religious ideals, while the lay/courtly culture develops on another track.

This is the endpoint of the Jean de Salisbury section. Physical p137 is blank.
