## High-value intellectual-history findings

### Twelfth-century `formation/fermentation`

The Paris 1930 lecture is not three parallel biographies. It is a composite analysis of civilisation. Huizinga treats the twelfth century as an `époque de formation` / `fermentation`, shaped by institutional and social mobility as well as classical/intellectual revival.

### Haskins and `naissance, non pas renaissance`

Huizinga explicitly engages C. H. Haskins's *The Renaissance of the Twelfth Century*. He accepts the importance of classical revival, translation and new learning but questions assigning that revival too much causal weight. Church reform, monasticism, chivalry, crusade, towns, schools and corporate forms belong to the causal architecture. The key conceptual result is `naissance, non pas renaissance`: a medieval Christian civilisation is being formed while absorbing antiquity.

### `primitive` as relational/pre-systematic

In the Abélard section `primitive` does not simply mean backward. Huizinga uses it for intellectual/social powers operating before mature methods, disciplinary restraints and systems have stabilised. `jeu`, `tournoi` and `disputa` belong to this competitive formative ecology.

### From `prégothique` to `esprit gothique`

The Alain section makes the transition explicit. `Prégothique` denotes a comparatively open, syncretic/pre-systematic condition; `esprit gothique` emerges with the pressure to order the world through categories, systems, harmony and style.

### Research practice as archival evidence

The Salimbene packet, Alain library-request apparatus and final Abélard/Bernard cards show Huizinga's method materially: bibliography, source acquisition, manuscript transmission, page concordance, quotation harvesting, source genealogy and synthetic interpretation coexist in one research workflow.
