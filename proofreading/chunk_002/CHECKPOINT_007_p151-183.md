## p151–173 — third `esprit`: Alain de Lille

The Alain section continues directly from p150 and develops into a sustained argument about colour, image, symbolic classification, realism and poetic form.

### p151–154 — colour and twelfth-century aesthetic sensibility

Huizinga compares several twelfth-century figures as different expressions of a new thirst for beauty/colour:

- Hugh of Saint Victor — mystical and symbolic perception of colour;
- Suger of Saint-Denis — architectural/material delight in gold, gems, stained glass and splendour;
- Alain de Lille — poetic colour, verbal `pictura`, and a highly coloured rhetoric.

The point is not simply that the century liked bright things. Huizinga treats colour as part of a broader renewal of sensory and imaginative form.

### p155–160 — systems of figures, symbolic order, and the Gothic turn

The lecture moves from colour to the organisation of ideas as figures and systems.

- Hugh of Saint Victor supplies examples of geometrical/symbolic ordering and encyclopaedic classification.
- Alain's attributed symbolic dictionaries / collections are brought into the same field.
- Huizinga discusses series of virtues/vices, liberal/mechanical arts and repeated groups of seven.
- p159 explicitly connects the liberal arts with the organisation of university knowledge and compares them with Hugh's mechanical arts.
- p160 gives the conceptual payoff: the need to reduce the world to categories and systems, to create order/harmony/style everywhere, is what Huizinga proposes to call the **`esprit gothique`**. The digression has therefore carried the argument from `prégothique` toward the formation of Gothic systematisation.

### p160–168 — Alain as poet of Realism / `pictura`

Huizinga returns to Alain and frames him as a poet of medieval **Realism** in the philosophical sense, opposed to nominalism and akin to Platonic idealism.

Using especially *Anticlaudianus* and *De planctu Naturae*, he follows:

- Nature and the Virtues as personified/real forces;
- the making of the perfect man;
- poetic prayer and encyclopaedic voyage;
- repeated vocabulary of `pingere`, `pictura`, image, sign, seal, garment, form and shadow;
- the sensible world as image/shadow of an intelligible world;
- painting/representation as a model for how form gives things their intelligibility.

Huizinga's interest is not only allegory as ornament. He treats resemblance, representation and generation/forming as closely connected operations in Alain's thought.

### p168–173 — pre-scholastic openness, courtly qualities, and conclusion

- p168 contrasts Alain's Platonising mode with later scholastic restriction and notes his relative distance from Aristotle.
- p169–172 compare Alain's allegorical worlds with later *Roman de la Rose* structures. Huizinga is especially interested in how virtues, courtly qualities and anti-courtly forces are grouped and personified.
- p172–173 provide the close: Alain is described as syncretic/eclectic and still `prégothique` because incompatible tendencies have not yet been forced into the tighter systematic and stylistic discipline associated with later Scholastic/Gothic culture.

The third `esprit` therefore ends at **p173**.
