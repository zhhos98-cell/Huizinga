## p70–75 — Abélard and Héloïse

p70 explicitly begins:

`Nous arrivons à Abailard.`

Huizinga first treats name/pronunciation, personality and the polarising character of Abélard. The biographical discussion rapidly reaches his poetry, *Sic et Non*, *Scito te ipsum*, the Paraclete and Héloïse.

p72 treats the correspondence/authenticity problem. Huizinga does not accept a simple fabrication solution; the letters/love story remain historical evidence for the emotional world under discussion.

p73–75 are important because he describes the Abélard–Héloïse relation as **`amour pré-courtois`**. Its directness, violence/absoluteness, sexuality, Christian consciousness of sin and conflict between faith and passion are distinguished from the later formalised system of courtly love. Huizinga even looks toward older/saga-like emotional forms rather than forcing the episode into a later literary code.

## p76–78 — book scarcity, voice, masters and schools

After the Héloïse episode Huizinga returns to Abélard as a type of an epoch.

The decisive setting is a manuscript-scarce society in which oral teaching and the personality of the master carry unusual force. Schools become intellectual `foyers`; masters and students move between them. The expansion of schools is treated as an institutional condition of twelfth-century intellectual life, not merely as background to doctrine.

## p79–81 — intellectual production as `jeu`, `tournoi`, `disputa`

Huizinga then introduces one of the strongest conceptual passages of the lecture: new literature/science/philosophy can emerge under the form of **`jeu`** — serious, competitive play.

He treats riddling, contest, wager and public intellectual performance as socially real forms. On p81:

- the knightly `tournoi`;
- the school/theological `disputa`

are parallel products of a combative age. Abélard himself is read through martial vocabulary: weapons of war are exchanged for dialectical arms; the travelling master resembles a public champion.

The comparison is structural, not decorative. It links pedagogy, prestige, competition and method.

## p82–85 — economy and rivalry of the schools

Famous dialectical masters earn money, attract mobile students and acquire celebrity. Scripture exposition itself can become a wager/tour de force.

Simon de Tournai supplies an example of intellectual virtuosity running into theological danger. Huizinga also follows Abélard's speed, `ingenium`, confidence, competitive challenge, school rivalries, envy, calumny and the use of logical traps.

The universals controversy is placed inside this ecology of competitive schools rather than narrated only as a sequence of doctrines.

## p86–89 — `primitive`, universals, *Sic et Non*, inquiry

p86 is quotation-sensitive. Huizinga explicitly characterises the ludic/competitive intellectual attitude as socially/psychologically **`primitive`**. The meaning in context is not simple backwardness. It marks an activity whose force and virtuosity have developed before stabilised disciplinary limits, methods and bodies of knowledge can reliably constrain it.

p87 turns to the problem of universals and Abélard's solution. Huizinga sees genuine innovation, but still within this formative/pre-systematic condition.

p88–89 treat *Sic et Non*: contradictions among authorities are deliberately assembled as an engine of doubt/inquiry. The familiar `Dubitando ... inquisitionem ... veritatem` formula belongs here, but any publication quotation should be checked against the image/source edition rather than raw OCR.

Huizinga compares the habit of inquiry with much later figures such as Francis Bacon while still refusing a Renaissance genealogy.

## p90–91 — `prégothique`, `primitif`, not Renaissance precursor

Huizinga lists traits that can look unusually liberal/rational/anti-superstitious, but then makes the classification explicit: **Abélard should not be treated simply as a precursor of the Renaissance.** He is `prégothique`.

p91 gives the comparative instruction: to understand the twelfth century, compare it with the century of Saint Thomas rather than using the Renaissance as the natural destination. `Primitif` here means over-early/formative — an intellectual type whose powers outrun the mature structures that will later organise the field.

This usage should be indexed as a major Huizinga `primitive` passage.

## p92–95 — Bernard, Erasmus, Jerome/Augustine temperaments

Saint Bernard and Abélard become opposite poles of one twelfth-century formation: religious concentration/authority versus premature or dangerously free dialectical virtuosity.

p93 takes Abélard's `esprit léger` and compares the hostility directed at such an urban, critical, literate intelligence with the later hostility around Erasmus.

p94–95 widen the typology through Saint Jerome and Saint Augustine. Huizinga is constructing recurrent Christian intellectual temperaments rather than claiming direct lineages:

- Abélard / John of Salisbury gravitate toward a literate, urban, critical/humanistic pole;
- Bernard / Hugh of Saint Victor toward a more Augustinian gravity and religious concentration.
