## p97–100 — explicit `deuxième`, biography and works

p97 begins with the explicit transition `deuxième` and introduces **Jean de Salisbury**. Huizinga warns that `prégothique` remains a heuristic category. He searches for a cultural type that might be described as a cultivated cleric who is not exhausted by a narrowly clerical identity (`clerc non-clérical` is the working field).

p98–100 establish the biography: English origin, study in France/Paris and school networks; service under Theobald of Canterbury; Thomas Becket; missions and Roman connections including Adrian IV; conflict/exile under Henry II; eventual bishopric of Chartres and death in 1180. Secure works include *Policraticus*, *Metalogicon*, letters and historical/hagiographical writing.

OCR p98 is effectively unusable and must never be cited as text evidence.

## p101–105 — character, fortitude, exile, service

Huizinga asks what kind of twelfth-century person John represents. He stresses a culture/intellectual type possible before later boundaries between cleric, courtier, scholar and gentleman have hardened.

p102–104 emphasise good humour, courage/fortitude and resilience in exile. Classical language of exile/patria coexists with Christian confidence. John is repeatedly contrasted with the more egocentric self-dramatisation Huizinga sees in Abélard.

p105 turns service and loyalty — especially to Theobald/Thomas Becket — into a central ethical feature. John is a counsellor/servant in institutional life, not merely a detached writer.

## p106–111 — `clerc-gentilhomme` and permeability of social forms

Huizinga develops the approximate type `clerc-gentilhomme`: clerical, courtly and chivalric forms still overlap.

Suger and Peter the Venerable are used as parallels. Peter's combination of noble bearing, gravity and `iocunditas`, his reception/protection of persecuted Abélard, and Thomas Becket's ecclesiastical vocation together with splendid/chivalric exterior all illustrate a world in which later estate/cultural separations are not yet complete.

Dress, monastic magnificence and chivalric habitus enter not as anecdotes but as evidence for this permeability. John can take chivalry seriously as a moral/social institution without becoming a lay knight.

## p112–119 — probability, doubt and firm religious belonging

The next cluster is epistemic. Huizinga highlights moderation and the refusal to force certainty where evidence/authority does not decide.

John's `Academic` disposition is treated as practical intellectual temper rather than a rigid doctrine: probability, suspension, comparison and willingness to leave some questions open.

Relics, inscriptions, testimony and miracle/tradition examples test the distinction between:

- firm Christian/ecclesiastical belonging;
- uncertainty about a particular historical claim.

Huizinga repeatedly notes that faith and local critical hesitation can coexist. This is crucial to his construction of John as a humane/critical type rather than a modern sceptic projected backwards.

## p118–120 — `humanités`, intellectual liberty, humour

John is characterised by broad `humanités`, varied knowledge, intellectual liberty and a clear/natural Latin style. Peter the Venerable's letters supply a related humane/playful register.

The *Policraticus* is valued for mixing classical exempla with observation/anecdote from contemporary life. Modern/social experience is allowed to function as evidence alongside books.

## p121–123 — ancients/moderns and outward knowledge

p121 contains the famous Bernard of Chartres image of **dwarfs on the shoulders of giants**. Huizinga uses it to define inheritance rather than a simple `Renaissance`: moderns may see farther because they stand on inherited antiquity, not because they have broken cleanly with the medieval world.

p122 stresses limits: admiration for Greek/Hebrew exists without easy direct access. Awareness of imperfect knowledge is itself part of the type.

p123 turns to **Peter the Venerable and the organised acquisition/translation of knowledge of Islam**, including the Latin Qur'an/Islamic translation project. Huizinga's interest is in a deliberate knowledge-making operation — securing materials, translation and intelligible information about the religion of the `infidèles` — rather than merely praising abstract tolerance.

## p124–125 — style and transition to political/social thought

Huizinga contrasts John's concise, living, nervous/clear Latin with the long-period rhetoric associated with later humanists. Humour in *Policraticus* is part of the same stylistic judgement.

p125 notes that style changes with office/subject — plain or modest in correspondence, dignified in official voice, more coloured/monumental in Becket material — and then opens the new subsection on John's political/social thought, continued in TEXT_PASS_007 p126–136.
