## Identity and scope

p76 opens a major continuous manuscript with the heading `1492–1559: de Nieuwe Tijd`. The unit continues through p150. pp.76–90 establish the period problem, survey modern historiography/sources, and then use Iberian expansion/discovery to show how the geographical and evidentiary horizon of European history changes.

## p76–78 — defining the `Nieuwe Tijd`

Huizinga does not define the early modern period through a single event. The opening works with several transformations at once: the modern state, cultural change, Reformation/Renaissance, shifts in political and economic centres, the rise of strong national/territorial states in western/northern Europe, the Habsburg-Spanish constellation, Italian political dispersion, French hegemony and English maritime power.

The Mediterranean/Roman centre is correspondingly relativised: Byzantium disappears, Ottoman power expands, Germany/Italy remain politically fragmented, and economic/maritime routes shift.

p77 contains self-reflective teaching notes about how to lecture this transition. Huizinga explicitly resists treating the material as a clean list of `new things`; the old medieval cultural field is itself still transforming and cannot simply be replaced at a date boundary.

p78 is partly historiographical/bibliographical control. Stable anchors include D. Schäfer, E. Fueter and Hauser/Renaudet on the beginnings of the modern age. Huizinga compares synthetic strategies — country-by-country versus interconnected presentation — and is alert to how a modern history textbook imposes coherence on uneven processes.

## p79–81 — the documentary regime changes

A major methodological passage asks how the **source base and forms of historical writing** change.

Huizinga notes the increasing availability and relevance of:

- archives (`archivalia`);
- coherent series of acts/records;
- letters, instructions and drawings;
- diplomatic instructions, reports and correspondence;
- Venetian `Relazioni`;
- papal nuncial reports;
- government records;
- pamphlets and news reports.

He then tracks changes in contemporary historical writing: older chronicle forms persist, but literary/humanist history, official history, memoirs, diaries, observations and personal accounts become increasingly important.

Named examples include Commines, Machiavelli and Guicciardini. The shift is not presented as simple progress toward objectivity. Humanist literary history may seek causes, coherence, style and political lessons while at the same time becoming rhetorical and distancing itself from raw documentary authority. Memoirs and diaries bring life/immediacy but also retrospective reconstruction, self-interest and selective memory.

The historiographical problem is therefore **relations among source forms**, not the victory of one superior genre.

## p82–85 — economy, geography and the conceptual problem of expansion

The manuscript shifts toward overseas expansion and asks how political/economic explanation should be connected to the rise of large commercial houses and capitalism without simply projecting a later system backward.

Huizinga then reconstructs geographical knowledge before Columbus. Stable names and anchors include:

- Paolo Toscanelli;
- Marco Polo/Cipangu;
- Amerigo Vespucci;
- Roger Bacon and older geographical traditions;
- Portuguese expansion along African routes;
- the relation between Mediterranean/Arabic geographical knowledge and Atlantic exploration.

The point is not a heroic `discovery` narrative. He is interested in what was known, miscalculated, transmitted and institutionally usable before the voyages.

## p86–88 — Columbus and the institutionalisation of conquest

The Columbus section is controlled through multiple historiographical traditions and documentary claims rather than a single biography. Huizinga notes Ferdinand Columbus, Las Casas and later historical accounts; he compares claims about Genoese origins, mercantile experience and Portuguese/Spanish contexts.

The sequence moves rapidly from voyage to institutional consequence:

- Iberian territorial division;
- colonial migration/settlement;
- importation of enslaved Africans;
- encomienda and Las Casas;
- Cortés/conquest;
- papal/legal-theological frameworks.

Thus `discovery` becomes a chain of navigation → claims → labour/settlement → administration → moral/legal controversy.

## p89–90 — world history and new information institutions

The manuscript extends through Magellan and the Casa de Contratación / Consejo de Indias. These institutions matter because they organise travel, information, administration and knowledge of overseas territories.

A stable closing movement states that the history of Europe increasingly opens into **world history**. Portugal and Spain also generate new historical/source genres — travel writing, reports, colonial histories, maps and institutional collections — that materially alter what historians can know.

## Research significance

pp.76–90 provide a compact statement of Huizinga's method for early-modern transition:

1. no single-event periodisation;
2. changing state, economy, religion, geography and media of knowledge are treated together;
3. new historical knowledge follows from new **documentary and administrative infrastructures**;
4. discovery is reconstructed as transmission, miscalculation, navigation, conquest and institutionalisation;
5. Europe becomes `world history` partly because routes and bureaucracies generate new kinds of evidence.
