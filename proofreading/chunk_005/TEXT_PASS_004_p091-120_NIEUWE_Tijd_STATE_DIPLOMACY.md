## `1492–1559: de Nieuwe Tijd` — modern state system, military/fiscal concentration, diplomacy and publicity

Status: **text_checked** (image-controlled argument pass)

## p91–94 — from dynastic conflict to a state system

Huizinga explicitly introduces the problem of the `typen`/formation of modern states and the institutions/tendencies of international politics.

He distinguishes older dynastic/feudal conflicts from a newer field in which multiple states can increasingly act as concentrated units. The key explanatory problem is not simply the appearance of `the state`, but the **concentration of forces, means and interests** that makes coordinated action possible.

The manuscript also resists a uniform Europe: older kingdoms, Habsburg combinations, Italian powers, the Empire and other territorial structures retain different constitutional forms and different degrees of centralisation.

## p95–98 — fiscal-military capacity

The argument becomes concrete through the means of war.

Huizinga notes:

- crown access to money/taxation;
- war becoming professional and expensive;
- hired troops and Swiss military labour;
- artillery and the technical transformation of siege warfare;
- marching, reconnaissance, provisioning and enlarged operational terrain;
- the relation between logistics and political capacity;
- Mediterranean naval power.

The underlying distinction is between **nominal sovereignty and usable capacity**. A ruler with titles but without credit, fiscal reach, troops, logistics and communications cannot act as a coherent state power.

## p99–102 — diplomacy as information infrastructure

This is one of the strongest passages in the chunk for the Huizinga institutional project.

The manuscript treats early-modern diplomacy as an organised information system. Stable components include:

- increasingly fixed/permanent diplomatic representation;
- envoys/ambassadors and secretaries;
- spies and systematic information gathering;
- reports and correspondence;
- courts as information nodes;
- Venice, France, Spain and England within the developing network.

Huizinga connects this directly to the growth of `publiciteit`/publicity:

- political pamphlets and humanist writing;
- publication of treaties and polemics;
- news circulation;
- rulers increasingly addressing audiences beyond immediate court negotiation.

Diplomacy and publicity are therefore not separate topics. They are two sides of an expanding political communication field: confidential information gathering and public political presentation.

## p103–104 — Machiavelli, political thought and reason of state

Machiavelli becomes the most obvious conceptual anchor, but Huizinga does not present him as the lone inventor of modern politics. The political thought is situated inside the practical environment already reconstructed: conflict, diplomacy, state capacity and shifting norms.

The notes also engage later conceptual historiography (including Meinecke) and the emergence of norms concerning interstate conduct/war. The Reformation further changes the political field because religious allegiance becomes increasingly territorial and bound to rulers/state structures.

## p105–110 — dynastic machinery and the material means of monarchy

Huizinga moves through dynastic claims, marriage, succession and the practical resources of monarchy.

He repeatedly returns to:

- arsenals and mobilisation;
- taxation/finance;
- the recruitment and maintenance of forces;
- administrative coordination;
- the difference between inherited claims and executable political power.

This section makes dynastic history material: genealogy matters only insofar as claims can be converted into alliances, offices, armies, money and territorial control.

## p111–117 — Spain: religion, administration and social form

The Spanish sequence uses Ferdinand/Isabella and Charles V to test a multi-factor explanation of consolidation.

Huizinga treats together:

- religious unification and coercion;
- the Inquisition/Moriscos;
- councils and administrative organs;
- the Comuneros;
- nobility/hidalgo social forms;
- dynastic inheritance;
- regional difference;
- economic and military resources.

The recurring methodological point is that dynastic, religious, economic and institutional factors have to be distinguished before they can be related. None is treated as a sufficient single cause.

## p118–120 — France, high nobility and absolutism

The sequence moves into French/Bourbon-Valois genealogy, apanages, high nobility and centralisation. `Absolutism` appears not as a timeless property of monarchy but as the outcome/claim produced through conflicts over territorial, dynastic and administrative power.

The pages also touch the language of universal monarchy and the problem of whether the concentration of authority actually overcomes older aristocratic/territorial structures.

## Research significance

pp.91–120 strongly support a relational reading of Huizinga's political history:

1. a `state` becomes effective through relations among finance, force, administration, information and territory;
2. military technology is inseparable from logistics, money and recruitment;
3. diplomacy is an **information practice**, not ornamental court politics;
4. publicity grows alongside diplomatic secrecy and organised reporting;
5. dynastic titles/claims have to be translated into operational capacity;
6. Spain is analysed through interacting but non-identical religious, social, administrative and economic mechanisms.
