## pp.154–157 — 1919 course sequence

### 30 September / early October 1919

The sequence opens with the political character of the sixteenth century and the relationship between Mediterranean/Roman and north-western European centres. Columbus/overseas discovery enters immediately as part of a broader reorganisation of political and cultural geography.

### October 1919

Subsequent dated prompts connect:

- Portuguese expansion and conquest;
- dynastic politics and European balance;
- military organisation;
- the relation of territorial power to economic resources;
- the changing scale of political conflict.

The notes are not merely chronological reminders. They repeatedly ask how a new political system is produced from relations among dynasty, military technique, trade/capital and territorial consolidation.

### November 1919

The later strips move more explicitly toward capital/economic form and the contrast between agrarian/natural economy and developing monetary/capital relations. Pirenne and related historiography appear as conceptual supports.

This 1919 outline should be read beside the preceding pp.152–153 post-war reflection: Huizinga is simultaneously questioning the purpose of historical teaching and rebuilding a course around structural relations rather than a simple succession of events.

## pp.159–164 — 1929–30 course sequence

A second compact series begins with dates in October 1929 and extends into spring 1930.

### Autumn 1929

Topics include:

- state, enterprise/economic life and culture;
- Portugal and overseas expansion;
- conquest and the character of colonial/imperial action;
- centralisation and recurring conflict among political powers;
- the institutional means of the modern state.

### Late 1929

The notes connect military organisation and economic structure, then return to religion/papal politics and territorial political change. The Netherlands, Switzerland and German territories appear as cases in a differentiated European field.

### 1930

The sequence increasingly treats `capital` as an explanatory problem rather than a label. Huizinga asks about market production, monopoly/public monopoly, rents/revenues, private versus public control and the relation between economic organisation and political capacity.

The compressed form shows him actively testing causal vocabulary (`factor`, cause/effect, market production, monopoly) rather than simply assigning the period to `capitalism`.
