## Internal development of the unit

### p81–90 — discovery, historical writing and the enlargement of the world

The argument moves from changing forms of historical testimony (`mémoires`, autobiography, reports, publication) into geographical expansion and the epistemic consequences of discovery.

Anchors include:

- Benvenuto Cellini and Cardanus among autobiographical/self-documenting forms;
- Toscanelli, Columbus, Amerigo Vespucci and the cartographic/geographical problem of reaching Asia westward;
- Portuguese and Spanish expansion, Atlantic/African routes and pre-Columbian geographical expectations;
- Las Casas, Garcilaso de la Vega, the Casa de Contratación / Consejo de Indias and colonial institutionalisation;
- the claim that European history becomes increasingly inseparable from world history through Iberian expansion and the resulting travel/reporting literature.

The OCR is often lexically corrupt, but the page order and named conceptual sequence are visually and contextually secure.

### p91–98 — the modern state system and the concentration of force

A new sub-section explicitly turns to the `typen` / formation of modern states and the international political system. Huizinga treats the concentration of military, fiscal and administrative means as historically constitutive rather than simply taking the sovereign state for granted.

Key themes:

- institutions and tendencies of international politics;
- dynastic conflict giving way to wider state-system interactions;
- concentration of `krachten` and the ability of states to act as coherent units;
- crown finance and taxation;
- war as increasingly professional/technical activity;
- Swiss military power, hired troops, artillery, logistics and the enlargement of operational terrain;
- maritime power and the strategic problem of Mediterranean control.

### p99–104 — diplomacy, publicity and political thought

This section gives unusually clear attention to diplomatic technique and the media/publicity environment of politics:

- permanent/fixed diplomatic representatives;
- envoys, secretaries, spies and information gathering;
- France, Venice, Spain and England as examples of developing diplomatic practice;
- humanists and political writers within diplomatic/publicistic networks;
- publication of treaties, polemic and the expansion of political publicity/public opinion;
- Machiavelli and the conceptualisation of political action/state reason;
- later historiographical framing, including Meinecke.

### p105–117 — dynastic monarchy, administration and Spain

The unit turns from interstate mechanism to monarchic machinery and dynastic/state consolidation. It tracks marriage, succession, arsenals, mobilisation, finance and court administration, then develops Spain through Ferdinand/Isabella, Charles V, councils, the Inquisition/Moriscos, the Comuneros and the social-political position of nobility/hidalgo forms.

Huizinga repeatedly distinguishes dynastic, religious, economic and administrative causal fields rather than allowing one factor to explain state formation by itself.

### p118–120 — France, centralisation and absolutism

The sequence moves into Bourbon/Valois genealogy, apanages, high nobility, centralisation and the political vocabulary of absolutism/universal monarchy. This section continues directly into the next review PDF.
