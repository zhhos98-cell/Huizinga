## p121–127 — territorial strategy and imperial/dynastic claims

The manuscript continues the modern-state analysis through French/Burgundian, Italian and imperial configurations. Huizinga treats territory strategically rather than as a passive map: particular regions matter because they connect routes, dynastic possessions, military fronts and competing claims.

A substantial bibliographical apparatus surrounds Charles V. The notes compare political narrative with constitutional/territorial history and keep the gap between imperial title and executable authority in view.

## p128–132 — the limits of Charles V's power

Huizinga emphasises how difficult it is to convert a composite Habsburg inheritance into concentrated rule.

The limits include:

- constitutional autonomy/estates in the Empire;
- territorial fragmentation;
- dependence on local resources and cooperation;
- recruitment and military-financial constraints;
- competing French/Ottoman fronts;
- religious division.

The Reformation is especially important because religious allegiance becomes more closely tied to territorial rulers and political structures. Huizinga treats this not simply as the `effect` of religion on politics but as a transformation in the relation between religious movement and territorial authority.

## p133–141 — differentiated European political fields

The manuscript compares the Habsburg Netherlands, Switzerland, Spain, England and eastern/northern Europe. These pages repeatedly ask what combination of conditions produces usable political power:

- territorial/geographical coherence;
- population and military labour;
- urban/economic structure;
- nobility and social hierarchy;
- external strategic position;
- religious conflict;
- administrative concentration.

Switzerland is treated through its military/political position and limits after major conflicts; Spain through territorial and military resources; England through the aftermath of the Wars of the Roses and Tudor consolidation. Political and religious history are explicitly inseparable in several northern/eastern cases.

## p142–148 — Russia, service structures, Byzantium and the Ottoman field

Huizinga broadens the comparison beyond the standard western-European state story. Russian historiography and service/social categories are noted, followed by Byzantine and Ottoman political forms.

The Ottoman section is concerned with concrete institutional capacity:

- service/administrative groups;
- diplomatic practice;
- standing military forms;
- centralisation;
- fiscal resources;
- relations with subject/elites;
- Byzantine/Phanariot continuities.

The comparison is analytical rather than a simple civilisational contrast. Huizinga asks which combinations of personnel, money, administration, military technique and hierarchy make centralised action possible.

## Overall argument of pp.121–150

The section tests the `modern state` concept against composite and non-uniform political structures. Huizinga's recurrent question is not `which country became modern first?` but:

**What relations among territory, estates, finance, military organisation, religion, social hierarchy, administration and information allow a ruler/political structure actually to act?**

This makes `state formation` a problem of coordination and capacity rather than a label attached to sovereignty.
