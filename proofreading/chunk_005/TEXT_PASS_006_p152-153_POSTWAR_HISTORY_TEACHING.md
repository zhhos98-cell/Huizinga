## p152 — `hoe moet ik verder geschiedenis onderwijzen?`

The first page begins from a personal/intellectual crisis about the teaching of history after the war. A stable central question is:

`hoe moet ik verder geschiedenis onderwijzen?`

Huizinga writes that he no longer understands the task in the old way and that attempts simply to read/teach a period do not reach what he is looking for.

The war has altered the weight of the past. Things once treated as secure/important historical inheritances now seem to have lost their old obviousness. He asks whether this is only a temporary disturbance or whether it exposes a deeper transformation in civilisation.

The page then lists the destructive symptoms around him: waste/destruction, breakdown of long-formed organisation, return of conditions thought overcome, and the erosion/confusion of concepts. Historical teaching is therefore posed inside a crisis of **civilisational continuity**, not merely a pedagogical problem.

## p153 — refusing despair, without restoring easy optimism

The second page opens with a stable injunction not to despair of humanity (`Niet wanhopen aan de menschheid`). But Huizinga explicitly does not return to a simple optimistic register.

The desired orientation is modest: not belief in perfectibility, but hope for a bearable earthly order, soundness and human attachment. He contrasts abstract ideals about humanity's capacity with the simultaneous spectacle of human smallness/childish folly and the astonishing good/valuable capacities given to human beings.

The closing returns to history: the long relation to `historia`, love for human beings, and attention to the things of the past remain possible after the collapse of easy progress narratives.

## Research significance

This two-page text is a compact primary witness to Huizinga's post-First-World-War reorientation.

1. **Teaching crisis as epistemic crisis:** the question is not how to update a syllabus, but what historical continuity means after civilisational rupture.
2. **No simple progress narrative:** the war destroys confidence in automatic civilisation/progress.
3. **No simple despair either:** historical work is relocated within a deliberately reduced, earthly and humane horizon.
4. **Chronological control:** the 1919/20 college-year setting makes this an early post-war document and should be indexed accordingly.
